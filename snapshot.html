              1. Introduction

              Processes (aka calculation, analysis modules) and agentic AI (part of Testbed 2026 phase 3, to be tendered later this year) are what set a Digital Twin of the Physical Living environment apart from a GIS or BI environment. Being able to simulate, analyze, predict and calculate are essential components to determine KPI’s so that policy makers can make informed decisions.

              Having open, proven, standards-based, and API-enabled (machine-first) processes allows the process (a calculation or analysis) to be taken up in workflows and automations. However, the human remains in the loop and takes the ultimate decision.

              1.1 Goal of the testbed

              We want to encourage the adoption of uniform interoperable standards for Digital Twins, based on the nLDT architecture (data, processes and visualization, see https://geonovum.github.io/NLDT-Architectuur/). In this Testbed, the focus is on interoperable processes. With these research and adoption topics, we want to create sufficient ‘critical mass’ – enough organizations in an ecosystem that work with the same interoperability arrangements. This will lead to an interoperable, networked system of digital twins for the physical environment.

              The adoption topics use established API specifications, that have a high level of maturity.

              The research topics do not have the same maturity level: here we are identifying (and eliminating) the obstacles for interoperability.

              Geonovum, in line with its mission, is keen to promote the use of standards, drive adoption and foster research; and it seeks to involve the market to do so. The actual questions and issues to be addressed are described in this document as research and adoption topics.

              1.2 Scope

              In this testbed we focus on implementing the OGC API Processes standard. There is no centralized use-case, we request the particpants to implement their own use case.

              1.3 Outcome

              The results of the testbed are intended to contribute to expanding and innovating the Dutch public sector Spatial Data Infrastructure in a direction that considers the possibilities in the market today. Based on the outcome of this testbed we may take steps to make the OGC API Processes standard mandatory within the Dutch public sector, to promote interoperability between digital twins and encourage reuse of processes and calculation models.

              2.4 Research topic #4: App-Store / Recipes

              2.4.1 Goal

              Offer ready-made solutions for real-world problems, expressed as a ‘recipe’: a combination of data-sources (ingredients) and operations in the form of an ordered list of processes (like cooking instructions).

              2.4.2 Description

              We are familiar with the App Store (and Play Store) from our mobile phones (smartphones, smart devices): here we find all kinds of applications ('Apps', applications) that, after installation, work immediately and are integrated with the smartphone ecosystem.

              The Windows and macOS operating systems also have an App Store – where apps are promoted but also organized into categories to make searching easier. Just like on smart devices, installation is flawless and the application works seamlessly within the operating system's ecosystem.

              Payment is easily arranged in both the App Stores of smart devices and those of Windows or macOS – digital payments can be made via all kinds of payment methods.

              App settings are stored in the operating system; when the app is used on another device by the same user, it is immediately recognizable. 

              Digital Twins also operate within their own ecosystem (and together form an ecosystem). Digital Twins are defined using computational modules and an App Store in the context of Digital Twins, it should offer easy-to-install-and-use computational modules.

              Whereas the App Store in smart devices uses the native functions of Windows or macOS, the App Store in Digital Twins uses HTTP as the primary means of communication.

              2.4.2.1 Recipes

              Recipes can be expressed in various ways. OGC API Processes part 2 https://docs.ogc.org/DRAFTS/20-044.html. Common Workflow Language (CWL), a standard that specifies the document schema and execution semantics for composing workflows from components such as command line tools and other workflows. This could be a way to describe recipes. Another proposal for recipe description was created within nLDT. This proposal is not yet standardized and could be used as a basis or inspiration source for experimental implementation. (see appendix B for link to the experimental implementation) A third option for describing recipes is Arazzo, a community-driven open specification within the OpenAPI Initiative. (https://www.openapis.org/arazzo-specification)

              2.4.2.2 Catalogs

              A catalog serves as a central hub where datasets and services are collected, documented, and made accessible. It offers a clear structure in which information is described via metadata, such as title, subjects, origin, and format. This allows users to quickly discover what data is available and how to utilize it, without having to search through separate documents or scattered sources.

              In addition to organizing data, a catalog offers functions for searching, filtering, and comparing services. Users can filter using search terms, categories, or tags to find exactly what is relevant to their application. The system often supports multiple types of services—from open data to internal administrative records—and can process various data types and files, such as tables, geographic data, or API connections.

              An important aspect of such a catalog system is the focus on accessibility and reusability. Through standardization, clear documentation, and reliable connections, it becomes easy to integrate data into analyses, applications, or visualizations. Moreover, the platform encourages collaboration between departments, organizations, or developers by making datasets findable and understandable, which contributes to transparency and innovation.

              2.4.2.3 Metadata

              Metadata consists of descriptive data that helps to find, understand, and correctly interpret datasets and services. They include information about the content, origin, structure, and quality of the data, among other things. By capturing this context, it becomes clear to users exactly what a dataset contains, how current or reliable it is, and in which situations it is suitable for use. In this way, metadata acts as a kind of user manual for data.

              Standards are often used to record metadata in a structured and interchangeable manner. A widely used standard is DCAT, a vocabulary developed to describe services. DCAT defines uniform terms and relationships, making services easier to publish, search, and link between different platforms. Through this standardization, organizations can keep their data descriptions consistent and promote interoperability.

              In addition to improving findability and consistency, metadata also contributes to transparency and governance. They clarify who is responsible for a dataset, which license applies, how it is updated, and what restrictions apply. As a result, users can better assess whether the data is suitable for their purposes, and organizations maintain an overview of their own information management.

              2.4.2.4 Recipe metadata

              DCAT is a mature and widely used standard, and the Netherlands has a standardized DCAT profile: DCAT-AP-NL. However, DCAT-AP-NL is designed to describe datasets and services. It does not currently support metadata for recipes: combinations of data sources and operations that together produce a result.

              For users to find recipes in a catalog, they must be described using metadata, just like datasets and services. How to capture recipe metadata in DCAT-AP-NL is not yet fully defined.

              There are standards extending DCAT to describe properties like Recipes. We are investigating DPROD (Data Product Ontology (DPROD)) and APKG (GitHub - ILIAD-ocean-twin/APKG: Application Package Application Profile · GitHub).

              3.6 Research topic #4: Collaboral

              Collaborall investigated how a recipe — a combination of data sources and an ordered list of processes — can be described, found and executed in a national digital-twin App Store, using OGC API Processes, OGC API Records and DCAT-AP-NL. We built the answer on ANT, our existing workflow platform, because that is the realistic case: an organisation adopts these standards on the platform it already runs.

              Our main findings and advice:

              1. A recipe needs its own place in DCAT-AP-NL. It is neither a dataset nor a data service. We propose a small extension — two classes and six properties — that catalogues a recipe beside the datasets and services it uses, so a catalogue can answer which recipes use this service or this dataset. The extension validates against the published DCAT-AP 3.0 and DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 shapes.
              2. A process and a recipe differ in visibility, not in structure. A process is a black box and is catalogued as an ordinary dcat:DataService; a recipe is a transparent composition whose steps can be inspected.
              3. Arazzo is not enough for recipes. It describes a sequence of API calls and nothing else of a workflow. We moved to the Open Workflow Specification (OWS); another awardee independently left Arazzo as well. We advise Geonovum to choose one recipe language and the subset of it a recipe may use.
              4. No standard and no platform carries a value from one step to the next. That wiring is described by the recipe language and has to be executed by whoever runs the recipe.
              5. A catalogue that conforms to the profile can still be rejected by a harvester. The harvester behind data.europa.eu refuses our valid catalogue because of the links that make a recipe a recipe. An extension needs to be tested against real harvesters, not only against shapes.
              6. Several recipes ask for the same plan in different shapes. A shared, published concept set for a scenario — an area and a building programme — would let one input serve many recipes. We built a proof of concept on an existing OGC keyword.

              3.7 Research topic #4: FutureInsight

              We built a working, end-to-end App Store for recipes: a catalogue (OGC API Records over CKAN), a Cookbook Registry, a Cook that is itself an OGC API Process, and a web client. Recipes are written in CWL v1.2 and executed against our own processes and external testbed processes (roofer, Geolabs ZOO-Project, Varidian).

              Our main conclusion: CWL works as a recipe format, but not unextended. CWL describes ingredients, order and wiring well. It cannot say where a step's OGC API Process lives, which alternative processes a hub may use, or describe process inputs as precisely as JSON Schema does. We added three small extension fields in a declared nldt: namespace to close those gaps; with them, all five bundled recipes pass the reference validator, cwltool --validate.

              Other key findings:

              • CWL's abstract Operation step maps one-to-one onto a remote OGC API Process.
              • CWL's own File + format cleanly separates data ingredients from parameters, and maps onto OGC by-reference (href) inputs.
              • OGC API Processes descriptions do not say machine-readably which inputs are datasets, so the recipe must declare it.
              • Asynchronous execution with job polling is mandatory in practice for long-running processes.
              • DCAT-AP-NL has no recipe concept yet; proposing the extension is the main open research item.
              • Writing CWL by hand is hard for non-specialists; a guided editor is essential.
              • Every part of the stack works from one plan: the Cookbook Registry turns a recipe into a DAG, and the editor, catalogue, Wizard, Status tab and Cook all ask it for that plan instead of reading the CWL themselves.
              • An OGC API Process description already carries most of what a user needs to fill in a parameter (title, description, default, limits); the Wizard reads it when it shows the form, so recipes need not copy it.

              3.9 Research topic #5: ThePeopleGroup

              The People Group implemented an exporter and an importer for Scene Context JSON (SCX) in its DigitalTwin platform and opened the exported scenes side by side in CesiumJS and ArcGIS. This report documents what we found, with the documents, code, measurements and service responses each finding is based on, and translates the findings into proposals for the SCX specification.

              The main conclusions:

              • The camera position travels, the lens does not. Position, heading and tilt were reproduced exactly in three engines. The visible area differed by almost a factor of two because each engine uses a different default and definition of the field of view. We added fov and propose projection type and clipping planes (1.2).
              • The nested interval structure is hard to implement and easy to get wrong. It required workarounds in a typed environment and our own exporter produced output that does not validate. We propose a simple { start, end } object (1.1).
              • A layer reference must be complete and typed unambiguously. An incomplete WMS URL produced two different pictures from one document, and independently written exporter and importer code already disagreed on format strings. We propose that url is the complete request and a closed type/format table (1.4, 1.5).
              • The same 3D Tiles give different results per viewer. All 1309 building tiles of one dataset contain invalid glTF that Cesium-based viewers accept and ArcGIS silently does not draw; heights stored as NAP but declared ellipsoidal cause a vertical offset of about 41 m in ArcGIS; instanced tree tiles are not supported by ArcGIS at all. We propose per-layer metadata for tile version, content type and vertical datum (1.6, 1.9).
              • Simulation results cannot be exchanged yet. 21 of the 28 resources in the test scene, including every time-dependent simulation result, have no stable URL another viewer can request. The 4D part of the scene therefore cannot be exported today (2.3).
              • Results between implementations are only comparable with a shared status vocabulary per layer (1.7).

              3.10 Research topic #5: uCrowds

              Crowd simulation helps event organizers evaluate pedestrian movement and identify potential safety risks before an event takes place. Temporary event infrastructure, such as stages, barriers, entrances, and exits, is typically designed in CAD applications. Crowd simulation modelers often need to manually recreate walkable areas, obstacles, and other relevant features from these designs. This process is time-consuming, introduces opportunities for errors, and must be repeated when event designs change.

              This research investigates how SCX, a lightweight format for exchanging scene information between applications, can be extended to support crowd simulation. The extension presented in this report adds semantic information to geographic data, allowing simulation applications to distinguish between features such as walkable areas, obstacles, entrances, exits, and queues.

              The research was carried out by uCrowds and AniVizzion. AniVizzion implemented the export workflow in SCAD Re-Event, while uCrowds implemented the import workflow in SimCrowds. The demonstration showed that event designs can be transferred directly from CAD into SimCrowds, where the relevant simulation objects and a navigation mesh are created without manual redrawing.

              The workflow automates construction of the simulation environment, although users must still connect simulation objects into pedestrian flows and configure simulation parameters. Further research could improve interoperability through shared semantic definitions, investigate applications in other simulation domains, and explore closer integration between CAD design and simulation.

              A.7 Findings: describing GeoJSON inputs and outputs

              Note that every description discussed in this section passes the OGC test suite and ogc-checker.

              A.7.1 The output schema could not be satisfied

              On host1, the processDescription generated by the ZOO-Kernel for the route output of routing has a schema that is defined as follows:

              "schema": {
  "oneOf": [
    { "type": "object" },
    { "type": "object" }
  ]
}

              oneOf means "exactly one of these". A GeoJSON object matches both branches, so it is rejected, as is any other value. A JSON Schema validator confirmed that even the route returned by the service is not valid against this schema.

              For this specific issue, the cause is internal to the ZOO-Kernel. As the process metadata declares two output formats, application/geo+json and application/json, the ZOO-Kernel then creates one branch per format, but as both are "type": "object", they all become "type": "object".

              This was not noticed before, because client applications do not validate results against the schema. Nevertheless, the schema also tells a client nothing about the data. In the PLANSPACE Simulator, the same pattern used on an input produces the "option 1" selector shown in Figure 6.

              A.7.2 A client convention that is not in the standard

              Based on our understanding of the PLANSPACE Simulator and our discussion with the IMAGEM team present during the second plugfest, it seems the client application searches for a single top-level element: contentMediaType in the schema. If it is not found, the client disregards the inputs/outputs definition and any inputs/outputs following it.

              In the process description What the client displays
              output with contentMediaType = application/geo+json a GeoJSON layer on the map
              input with contentMediaType = application/geo+json drawing on the map
              contentMediaType = image/tiff; application=geotiff a GeoTIFF layer on the map

              The client application does not look at type or format, and does not follow $ref references either. To know what the user may draw, it requires the GeoJSON type names to be directly available within the schema.

              This differs from our understanding of the standard, where process descriptions use JSON Schema, in which contentMediaType applies to strings only and has no effect on objects. The examples of the standard use contentMediaType only on strings. For GeoJSON carried as a JSON object, they use the format key, with the geojson-feature-collection, geojson-feature and geojson-geometry values.

              The schema that the client expects would pass validation, because the meta-schema of the standard accepts both keywords. Consequently, servers and clients in the testbed agreed on a convention that works between them, but not the one defined by the standard.

              The test with routing1 confirms it. Its description is valid and passes ogc-checker:

              "schema": {
  "oneOf": [
    {
      "type": "object",
      "$ref": "https://schemas.opengis.net/ogcapi/features/part1/1.0/openapi/schemas/featureGeoJSON.yaml"
    }
  ]
}

              The client shows no input for this process, not even the plain number input that it displays correctly for StageAHN. The whole form fails. The likely cause is the reference to an external schema, which the form generator of the client does not resolve. This has not been verified inside the client.

              A.7.3 The point inputs were plain strings

              The startPoint and endPoint inputs were strings, and nothing in the description indicated that they had to follow the lon,lat format. A client application can only offer a point picker on a map if the input is described as GeoJSON.

              A.8 Proposal

              As a solution, GeoLabs would propose to describe GeoJSON values the way the standard intends, and to update the ZOO-Kernel accordingly, to provide a schema containing:

              • an object branch with the format key and a $ref to the official GeoJSON schema;
              • a string branch with contentMediaType, where JSON Schema gives it a meaning.

              The schema definition below for the route output is intended to make the process work with IMAGEM’s PLANSPACE simulator. Providing such a schema definition from a process description generated by the ZOO-Project is out of scope from the testbed perspective.

              "schema": {
  "oneOf": [
    {
      "allOf": [
        { "format": "geojson-feature-collection" },
        { "$ref": "https://geojson.org/schema/FeatureCollection.json" }
      ]
    },
    { "type": "string", "contentMediaType": "application/geo+json" }
  ]
}

              Below is the proposed schema for the two point inputs, which would take a GeoJSON point instead of a lon,lat string.

              "schema": {
  "oneOf": [
    {
      "allOf": [
        { "format": "geojson-geometry" },
        { "$ref": "https://geojson.org/schema/Point.json" }
      ]
    },
    { "type": "string", "contentMediaType": "application/geo+json" }
  ]
}

              Both schemas were checked using a JSON Schema validator and the official GeoJSON schemas:

              Value Current output schema Proposed output schema Proposed input schema
              The route, as an object Rejected Valid —
              The route, as a string Rejected Valid —
              A GeoJSON Point, as an object Rejected Rejected Valid
              A GeoJSON Point, as a string — — Valid
              A GeoJSON LineString, as an object — — Rejected

              In WPS, distinguishing between input and output formats/types was based on XSD schemas that we could associate with mimeType. Now, with OGC API - Processes - Part 1: Core, the metadata for a process must define the JSON schema for each input and output. This can be explained by the media type, which cannot, on its own, distinguish a feature collection from a feature or a geometry. The ZOO-Kernel must try to avoid producing identical branches, add the format value, and keep contentMediaType for strings. One can note that the following format choice supported by the input and output of a process is correctly exposed for binary data, where the binary data is transferred as a base64-encoded string and embedded in the extended-schema associated with the schema for a given input/output. The routing1 process deployed on host2 is a first step: it references GeoJSON schemas and has a single branch, but does not yet include the format key or the string branch.

              Changes for client applications. Changing the inputs to GeoJSON breaks the current callers, including the NWB Route Explorer, which will have to send points such as {"type": "Point", "coordinates": [5.364654, 52.160784]}. The route itself does not change.

              What is not proposed. The PLANSPACE Simulator would still not work with these schemas. A server could add "contentMediaType": "application/geo+json" at the top of the schema only for this client. As this keyword has no meaning on an object in JSON Schema, it is left out. It makes more sense for the client to recognise format and follow $ref, so that any description following the standard works.

              B. Topic 4: App-Store / Recipes — Collaborall

              DTaaS Testbed 2026, phase 2 · research topic #4 · findings report

              B.1 Summary

              Collaborall investigated how a recipe — a combination of data sources and an ordered list of processes — can be described, found and executed in a national digital-twin App Store, using OGC API Processes, OGC API Records and DCAT-AP-NL. We built the answer on ANT, our existing workflow platform, because that is the realistic case: an organisation adopts these standards on the platform it already runs.

              Our main findings and advice:

              1. A recipe needs its own place in DCAT-AP-NL. It is neither a dataset nor a data service. We propose a small extension — two classes and six properties — that catalogues a recipe beside the datasets and services it uses, so a catalogue can answer which recipes use this service or this dataset. The extension validates against the published DCAT-AP 3.0 and DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 shapes.
              2. A process and a recipe differ in visibility, not in structure. A process is a black box and is catalogued as an ordinary dcat:DataService; a recipe is a transparent composition whose steps can be inspected.
              3. Arazzo is not enough for recipes. It describes a sequence of API calls and nothing else of a workflow. We moved to the Open Workflow Specification (OWS); another awardee independently left Arazzo as well. We advise Geonovum to choose one recipe language and the subset of it a recipe may use.
              4. No standard and no platform carries a value from one step to the next. That wiring is described by the recipe language and has to be executed by whoever runs the recipe.
              5. A catalogue that conforms to the profile can still be rejected by a harvester. The harvester behind data.europa.eu refuses our valid catalogue because of the links that make a recipe a recipe. An extension needs to be tested against real harvesters, not only against shapes.
              6. Several recipes ask for the same plan in different shapes. A shared, published concept set for a scenario — an area and a building programme — would let one input serve many recipes. We built a proof of concept on an existing OGC keyword.

              The recommendations are collected in chapter 9, and a field reference for the recipe extension is in appendix A.

              B.2 1. Introduction

              B.2.1 1.1 The question

              The testbed asked how ready-made solutions for real-world problems can be offered as recipes in an App Store for digital twins. The topic names three mandatory standards — OGC API Processes, OGC API Records and DCAT-AP-NL — and observes that DCAT-AP-NL can describe datasets and services, but not yet recipes.

              We split that into four questions:

              • Describe. Which metadata does a recipe need, and how does it fit DCAT-AP-NL?
              • Find. How are recipes, processes and data published so that they can be searched together, also across the catalogues of several parties?
              • Execute. In which language is a recipe written, and what is needed to run one whose steps are OGC API Processes services?
              • Adopt. What do these standards ask of an existing platform that was not built for them?

              B.2.2 1.2 The use cases

              We worked from questions a municipality asks when it plans housing:

              • What does a housing programme do to an area? A user draws a plan area and enters a programme. The recipe first describes the area from national open data — the BAG and the CBS neighbourhood figures, read live from PDOK — and then projects the programme onto it: the effect on the household and age mix, on the split between price segments, and on the primary schools within reach. Facts and projection are two separate processes, so that the projection can be challenged without doubting the facts.
              • Is there room on the electricity grid? The same area and programme are checked against the national grid-capacity map of Netbeheer Nederland: which feeding areas are affected, what their published congestion status is, and what the development adds to peak demand.
              • Which planned projects collide? Project boundaries are filtered by area and period, and the remaining ones are tested for overlap in space and time. This two-step recipe was our first, on synthetic data.

              Five recipes over five processes are published. Two of them chain more than one step.

              B.2.3 1.3 The approach

              Every part of the solution is exposed through the standards the topic names:

              Deliverable What it is Standard
              Recipe metadata A recipe:Recipe profile on DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 DCAT-AP-NL, SHACL
              Catalogue Recipes, processes and datasets, searchable OGC API Records, DCAT-AP-NL
              Cookbook Each recipe as an executable document Arazzo, Open Workflow Specification
              Processes The steps a recipe calls OGC API Processes
              Client Recepten: find, fill in, execute, show on the map —

              The surface runs on ANT without changes to its core. Each endpoint is a configured endpoint of the platform that returns the document the standard requires, and a small proxy in front supplies the parts of HTTP the platform cannot set itself (chapter 6). The pattern is not specific to ANT.

              B.3 2. Recipe metadata in DCAT-AP-NL

              B.3.1 2.1 Why a recipe is not a dataset

              DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 describes a dcat:Dataset, which is data, and a dcat:DataService, which is an endpoint that serves or processes data. A recipe is neither: it is an executable composition over them.

              A recipe can be catalogued today without any extension, by calling it a dataset with one distribution. We advise against it. It tells every consumer that the resource holds data it does not hold, it brings in mandatory dataset properties that mean nothing for a set of instructions, and — most importantly — it hides what a user searches on: which services does this chain call, and which data does it need? The practical reason to catalogue recipes is to answer which recipes depend on this service and which recipes work on this dataset. A recipe typed as a dataset cannot answer either without the consumer fetching and parsing the recipe itself.

              B.3.2 2.2 The choices

              A recipe is a dcat:Resource with its own class. dcat:Resource is the class DCAT provides for catalogued things that are not datasets. It carries identification, rights and themes without claiming anything about data. A recipe therefore sits in the catalogue beside datasets and services, not disguised as one of them.

              A step points at the service it calls, as a dcat:DataService. This is the choice that matters most. It makes an OGC API Processes endpoint a catalogue entry in its own right rather than a URL hidden inside a document, and it creates the link that answers both search questions above.

              The metadata says that steps depend on each other, not how. We considered typed inputs and outputs per step in the metadata and left them out. The recipe document already states how one step's output feeds the next, and a second copy in the metadata can disagree with it. The metadata records only that a step uses another step's output; the recipe document stays the authority on the wiring.

              The language of the recipe is a metadata value, not part of the model. dct:conformsTo on each distribution names the language the recipe is written in. This was tested by events: when we moved from Arazzo to OWS (chapter 4), the model did not change. Each recipe now has two distributions, one per language, and a client reads dct:conformsTo to choose the one it can run.

              The extension is two classes and six properties. Appendix A lists them with their cardinalities.

              B.3.3 2.3 Process and recipe

              At the first plugfest the participants discussed what distinguishes a process from a recipe. A workflow can contain a workflow, so from the outside every recipe is a process. We therefore draw the line at visibility:

              Process Recipe
              What it is The deepest level a party offers A combination of processes
              Visibility A black box: inputs and outputs only Transparent: its steps can be inspected
              Supplied by Anybody, including other parties Composed from what is in the catalogue
              In DCAT dcat:DataService conforming to OGC API Processes recipe:Recipe

              A process is catalogued as a plain dcat:DataService, not as a class of our own. A class of our own would make every process invisible to a catalogue that does not know our vocabulary. With a plain data service, a recipe can call another party's process, and a harvester that has never heard of recipes still finds the process.

              One further rule follows. The housing recipe reads national open data that we do not publish. Our catalogue does not describe those datasets, because describing a dataset in a catalogue presents the catalogue's publisher as its publisher. The sources are named in the output of each process instead.

              A recipe, as we use the word, combines API processes into one answer and runs in seconds to minutes. When a person has to decide how the work proceeds, the chain becomes an organisational workflow, which depends on how an organisation works rather than on which services exist. That belongs in the organisation's own workflow tooling, which can call a recipe as one of its steps.

              B.3.4 2.4 What is not covered yet

              • Typing of ingredients. The extension has no terms for the object type, units, coordinate reference system or temporal validity of an ingredient. Existing vocabularies are the likely source (qudt:unit, dct:conformsTo, dct:temporal), and we did not want to invent terms that already exist. In practice the client takes its typing from the input schema of the recipe, not from the catalogue.
              • Nesting, execution mode and short human input. For the plugfest we proposed, as a basis for discussion, to let a step be a recipe itself, to state whether a recipe completes within the caller's session, and to model a short input from a person during a run — a confirmation, a choice between two results — as an asynchronous process. P-Plan, a W3C community vocabulary, has terms for plans and nested steps. None of this is built.
              • Other vocabularies. The topic description names DPROD and APKG. We read both and used them as inspiration, but adopted neither.
              • A media type for the recipe document. DCAT-AP requires the format of a distribution to come from an authority list. Neither Arazzo nor OWS is in it, so the distribution can only state application/json.
              • The namespace. Our vocabulary's namespace does not resolve. For this research that made no difference. It matters once several parties publish recipes — see below.

              B.3.5 2.5 One vocabulary, many publishers

              Two kinds of namespace are easily confused. The vocabulary — the recipe class and its properties — should exist once. If every publisher defines the same class under its own namespace, there are as many classes as publishers, and no catalogue can search across them. The identifiers of what is catalogued — a recipe, a process record — belong to the publisher, and a platform can provide a namespace for those to each of its customers, so that nobody has to set one up in order to take part.

              B.3.6 2.6 Advice from validating

              We validate the catalogue that is actually served — not only an example file — against both the DCAT-AP 3.0 and the DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 shapes, automatically on every change. Three things are enforced by the shapes and easy to miss in the text of the profile:

              • dcat:contactPoint is mandatory on a catalogue;
              • a catalogue must contain at least one dataset;
              • every referenced term — licence, standard, theme, language — must carry a type; a bare URI is a violation.

              Validating only against the national shapes misses the European rules that come with them.

              B.4 3. Catalogue and Cookbook

              B.4.1 3.1 What is published

              Endpoint Standard Content
              Records collection OGC API Records One record per released recipe, with its spatial extent and links to the recipe document and its DCAT description; search on text, bounding box and limit
              DCAT catalogue DCAT-AP-NL 3.0, JSON-LD Recipes, the processes they call and the datasets they use
              Cookbook — The list of recipes, and each recipe as an Arazzo or an OWS document
              Federated catalogue DCAT-AP The records harvested from other catalogues (3.4)

              Recipes, processes and datasets are together in the DCAT catalogue. The Records collection holds the recipes only, so one search across all three exists as one DCAT document and not yet as one Records search.

              B.4.2 3.2 A recipe is data, not code

              A recipe is stored as rows in two tables: one row per recipe and one per step. All documents — the Records item, the DCAT description and the recipe document in both languages — are generated from those rows. Publishing a recipe is therefore adding rows and setting its status to released; no code is written and nothing is deployed. Because every document comes from the same rows, the metadata and the recipe cannot disagree.

              We consider this the right shape for an App Store: someone who is not a developer can publish a recipe, and the store can list and manage what it holds. It asks one discipline of the publisher: a released recipe is replaced by a new version, never edited, because clients may already depend on it.

              B.4.3 3.3 What a persistent identifier has to be

              A catalogue entry promises that what it names will be the same tomorrow. That rules out pointing at something its owner can still edit. What a catalogue cites, and what a recipe step binds to, has to be a released, immutable version with its own stable address.

              ANT already had such a unit, because a released version is what its store offers to customers. This was the most useful match we found between an existing platform and the standards: a store that versions what it offers has already done most of the work of persistent identification.

              B.4.4 3.4 One catalogue over several

              An App Store with recipes from several parties needs one place to search, without one party becoming the authority over the records of the others. We followed the pattern that data spaces and data.europa.eu use: every party publishes its own catalogue, and a federated catalogue harvests them.

              • A register lists the catalogues to harvest. Three kinds of source are read: a DCAT catalogue, an OGC API Records collection, and an OGC API Processes server, whose processes are recorded as dcat:DataService.
              • A harvested record keeps its original identifier and its publisher. A record that disappears at the source is marked as gone only after a complete read of that source.
              • Personal contact details are removed before a record is stored.

              It was exercised against a public OGC demo server: its processes and a collection of about three hundred metadata records.

              B.4.5 3.5 A harvester refuses the catalogue

              If recipes are to travel as open data does — from a store to a national portal and on to data.europa.eu — the harvester behind data.europa.eu, piveau, must be able to read them. We ran it against our catalogue:

              Catalogue variant Result in piveau
              As served Refused as a whole; nothing is imported, not even the datasets and services
              Without the recipes Datasets and services imported
              With recipes, without their links to steps and ingredients Everything imported

              The recipe class is not the problem. The two links that make a recipe a recipe — to its steps and to its ingredients — each stop the whole import. Our catalogue is valid DCAT-AP-NL and unusable for the harvester that matters most.

              Advice. Conformance to a profile does not predict what a harvester does with an extension of it. A store that wants its recipes harvested should keep the catalogue entries to DCAT-AP properties and offer the structure of the recipe behind a distribution — the recipe document already is one. How an extension should carry structure is a question the profile should answer once, rather than each publisher discovering it.

              B.5 4. Recipe language and execution

              B.5.1 4.1 From Arazzo to the Open Workflow Specification

              We started with Arazzo, one of the three languages the topic proposes. An Arazzo step names an operation in an OpenAPI description and passes values between steps with expressions. For a simple chain of OGC API Processes calls that works well, and our first recipe ran end to end this way.

              It stops there. Arazzo describes a sequence of calls and nothing else of a workflow: no branching, no iteration, no parallel steps, no structured error handling and no nested workflows. This was also the conclusion of the first plugfest, and Varidian reports the same experience and likewise chose another language. Two alternatives were raised there. CWL is built around command-line tools and containers, and openEO around the predefined processes of one back-end. A recipe step is neither: it is a call to a service of another party.

              We chose the Open Workflow Specification (OWS 1.0.3, the CNCF workflow language formerly called Serverless Workflow) for three reasons:

              • calling an external HTTP or OpenAPI service is a first-class step;
              • it describes a whole workflow, including branching, iteration, parallel steps, error handling and nested workflows;
              • a check on the result of a step is a schema on its output, which is stricter than a comparison and fails with a typed error.

              The choice of language did not affect the metadata (2.2), and the Cookbook serves every recipe in both languages.

              Recipes from parties that use Arazzo can be translated to OWS when they are imported: a sequence becomes a list of tasks, each step a call and each expression a jq expression. CWL and openEO do not translate this way. Such an import is proposed, not built.

              One gap in OWS concerns OGC API Processes directly. A call bound to an OpenAPI operation takes an operation name and parameters; the specification does not say how a request body is passed. Every OGC process execution is a POST with a body. An OWS recipe over OGC processes therefore has to call a URL directly, and loses the binding through the API description that lets a stranger execute it.

              B.5.2 4.2 The missing mechanism: from one step to the next

              OGC API Processes describes and executes each step. The recipe language says how steps are connected. A workflow platform can order tasks, start them and record each run. None of them executes the connection itself: taking the output of one step and making it the input of the next.

              In our solution the executor does this — the client, in its own memory. That is faithful to the recipe, and it has consequences:

              • How a recipe runs depends on the client. Two clients that implement the expression language differently can run the same recipe differently. This is the strongest reason to agree on one language and the subset a recipe may use.
              • An executor should refuse what it does not implement, before the first call. Our client implements the part of OWS a recipe needs and refuses the rest up front. A half-executed recipe is worse than a refused one.
              • A binding that does not resolve is an error. A misspelled reference between steps must stop the recipe. Treated as an absent optional value, it produces an empty result that looks like a valid answer.
              • Asynchronous execution is a property of a process, not of a recipe. A recipe language can wait for an asynchronous step, or run two steps in parallel and continue when both are finished. Whether a step can run asynchronously is decided by the process server.

              B.5.3 4.3 Binding through the API description

              A recipe step should name an operation in a published API description, not a hard-coded address. Only then can someone who did not write the recipe execute it. This is easy to skip: our own executor knew where the processes were, so the recipe ran while the description it referred to was not yet published. We now publish the OpenAPI description, generated from the process descriptions, and the executor refuses a step whose operation the description does not declare.

              B.5.4 4.4 Calling the processes of other parties

              The client can connect to any OGC API Processes server and run each of its processes as a recipe of one step. Tested against a public demo server, this showed a practical obstacle: the server's CORS settings did not allow the Content-Type header, so a browser cannot send it a JSON execute request in the normal way.

              Advice. A process server that is to be called from a browser-based client must allow Content-Type in its CORS preflight. OGC API Processes does not mention it, and most servers are configured for server-to-server use.

              B.6 5. The client and the demonstration

              Recepten is the client shown in the demo video. It follows the flow the topic asks for: find the metadata, bind the recipe, execute it, and show the result in the viewer.

              1. Find. The catalogue lists the recipes, including those from federated sources.
              2. Fill in. The input form is built from the input schema of the recipe. Areas are drawn on the map.
              3. Execute. The recipe runs step by step, with the progress of each step shown.
              4. Show. Results appear as map layers, key figures and tables, as the recipe declares.

              B.6.1 5.1 One scenario for several recipes

              A user who has checked grid capacity for a plan wants to run the housing recipe on the same plan. Without further agreement that means drawing the area again and entering the dwellings again, in a different shape, because each process describes the same programme with its own fields.

              We found that the same building programme is collected at least three times in the Netherlands — for the housing monitor, for the grid operators, and now by recipes — and that, as far as we could find, none of these is published as an open information model with resolvable concepts.

              As a proof of concept we annotated the inputs of our processes with x-ogc-definition, a keyword from an approved OGC standard (OGC API – Features – Part 5: Schemas) that points an input at the concept it carries. With that annotation:

              • one scenario — an area and a programme — fills the housing recipe and the grid recipe, and the client asks only for what a recipe still lacks;
              • a fictitious third party with its own field names and its own words for values is filled from the same scenario without mapping code.

              The mechanism works on standards that exist today. The content does not exist yet: the concepts are ours, and they have not been agreed with the parties that own the definitions.

              B.7 6. The standards on an existing platform

              B.7.1 6.1 Where the standards and the platform meet

              Producing the documents the standards require was not difficult. What an existing platform often cannot express is the HTTP around the document:

              The standard needs Typical platform How we bridged it
              Query parameters (limit, bbox, q) Only a request body reaches the logic A proxy rewrites the request
              A specific media type (application/geo+json) Always application/json The proxy sets it
              Status codes (201, 400, 404 with an exception document) 200 or 500 Partly by the proxy; a refusal is a 200 with an exception document, declared as a deviation
              A Location header for a new job Not available Not bridged; the job document carries its own link
              Deferred, asynchronous execution with job resources Every request runs to completion Synchronous execution only is declared

              A platform that can declare a media type and a status code per endpoint needs no proxy. One that cannot, can still offer the full surface, at the cost of a proxy that is part of the conformant surface and needs tests of its own.

              For a platform to carry recipes natively, three things are needed: a released, immutable version of each endpoint (3.3), a read path for discovery documents that is cheap, and a way to pass the output of one step to the next (4.2).

              B.8 7. Collaboration and plugfests

              The plugfests changed our design more than any other input.

              • Plugfest 1 (28 September). The discussion on Arazzo led to our move to OWS (4.1). The discussion on what a process is, as opposed to a recipe, gave the distinction by visibility (2.3). And the wish to search several stores as one led to the federated catalogue (3.4). We had prepared a public surface, a Postman collection and a worked example for the other participants.
              • Plugfest 2 (27 October). In preparation our processes were extended to accept inputs as a value, as a qualified value and by reference, as OGC API Processes allows, and the client was extended to run the processes of other servers (4.4).

              No recipe was exchanged between the topic 4 participants. Our client can run the processes of another server and our catalogue can harvest another catalogue, but both were exercised against a public demo server and not against another participant. Three things stood in the way, and they would stand in the way again:

              • we did not receive the endpoints of another participant's recipes or catalogue, and our own surface was not yet on a stable public address;
              • the participants did not settle on one recipe language — we use OWS, Varidian reports WDL — so there was no recipe both sides could run;
              • each participant's own chain had to be finished first, and nothing in the schedule required both sides to be ready at the same moment.

              A jointly written description of the interface between a recipe step and an OGC API Processes service was not produced. From our findings its content would be: how an input is passed, how a refusal is recognised, what a browser client needs (4.4), and how an input states what it means (5.1).

              B.9 8. Conclusions

              A recipe can be described in DCAT-AP-NL with a small extension, found through OGC API Records and executed over OGC API Processes services. The three mandatory standards are sufficient for the parts they cover. What they leave open is what lies between them:

              • Between catalogue and recipe: DCAT-AP-NL has no place for a composition of services, and an extension that adds one is not automatically readable for harvesters.
              • Between the steps of a recipe: no standard executes the passing of a value from one step to the next. A recipe language describes it, and the three participants in this topic ended up with at least two different languages.
              • Between recipes: each recipe defines its own inputs, so the same plan has to be entered again for each one.

              An existing platform can offer all of this without changes to its core, with a small proxy for the parts of HTTP it cannot set.

              B.10 9. Recommendations

              B.10.1 For Geonovum

              1. Choose one recipe language and the subset of it a recipe may use. Today the way a recipe runs is defined by whichever client executes it. We recommend the Open Workflow Specification, with Arazzo recipes translated on import (4.1, 4.2).
              2. Keep the recipe vocabulary in one shared namespace. The classes and properties should exist once, kept by a party that outlives any single participant. Identifiers of recipes and processes belong to their publishers, and a platform can provide those to its customers (2.5).
              3. Make the scenario — an area and a programme — a building block of the nLDT. Publish the concept set for a building programme, with the parties that collect it today as owners, and derive an exchange format from it that an OGC process can refer to. A catalogue can then answer which recipes can run on my scenario? (5.1).
              4. Test a recipe store against a real harvester. Conformance to the profile is necessary and not sufficient (3.5).
              5. Make exchange between participants a planned milestone, with a date, endpoints exchanged beforehand and a stable public address for each participant (chapter 7).
              6. Have the interface between a recipe step and a process service written down, jointly by the builders of services, clients and recipes (chapter 7).

              B.10.2 For the DCAT-AP-NL working group

              1. Give compositions of services a recognised place in the profile. A recipe is neither a dataset nor a data service (2.1). Appendix A is our proposal.
              2. State how an extension should carry structure, so that harvesters do not refuse the whole catalogue (3.5).
              3. Mention in the text what the shapes enforce, and advise publishers to validate the catalogue they serve against both the European and the national shapes (2.6).
              4. Provide for the media types of workflow documents (2.4).

              B.10.3 For the OGC

              1. Describe what an existing platform minimally needs to offer OGC API Processes, and what a proxy may supply (6.1).
              2. Let a server declare that a client can poll a job without the work being deferred, so that platforms that run every request to completion can state honestly what they do (6.1).
              3. Recommend that process servers allow Content-Type in their CORS settings (4.4).
              4. Let the test suite follow links instead of assuming a path layout (6.2).

              B.10.4 For the Open Workflow Specification

              1. Specify how a call bound to an OpenAPI operation passes a request body (4.1).
              2. Register a media type, so that a catalogue can tell a workflow document from other JSON (2.4).

              C.1 Summary

              We built a working, end-to-end App Store for recipes: a catalogue (OGC API Records over CKAN), a Cookbook Registry, a Cook that is itself an OGC API Process, and a web client.               C.4.1 One cook run, step by step

              A run has five stages: find the recipe, bind it in the Wizard, start the cook job, execute the steps, and read the status and result.

              1. The client lists recipes from the Records Wrapper (GET /collections/recipes/items). Each record links to its CWL in the Cookbook Registry.
              2. The client asks the Registry for the recipe's input schema and DAG, lists matching Hub Datasets, and the user binds tools, data and parameters in the Recipe Wizard ([#the-recipe-wizard]).
              3. On the Status tab, Execute starts the cook process with {recipeId, parameters, toolOverrides}, asking for asynchronous execution, and gets a job back straight away.
              4. The Cook fetches the DAG from the Registry and executes each step in dependency order, independent steps in parallel, piping outputs into the next steps' inputs ([#what-happens-when-you-click-execute]).
              5. The client polls /jobs/{id} every 2 seconds, showing each step's progress, then reads the result plus a per-step trace. The result is saved to Hub Datasets automatically.

              C.4.2 Design principle: one reader of the recipe, one plan for everyone

              A recipe is written once, as CWL, and then used in many places: the editor checks it, the catalogue shows it, the Wizard explains it, the Status tab summarises it and the Cook runs it. If each of those read the CWL itself, each would need its own CWL parser, and sooner or later two of them would disagree about what a recipe means.

              So only the Cookbook Registry reads CWL. Everything else asks the Registry one question, "what is the plan for this recipe?", and gets back the same kind of answer: the recipe's DAG (directed acyclic graph) plus the JSON Schema of its inputs. Whether the reason for asking is to check a draft, explain the recipe to a person, draw a picture or run it, the answer is the same plan.

              Why a DAG, and not the CWL itself? CWL is written for people. Steps appear in whatever order the author wrote them, and the file carries labels, descriptions, comments and extension fields. The machine that runs a recipe needs something else: which step must wait for which, and where each input of each step comes from. For example, a step written first in the file may need the output of a step written second. In CWL that dependency is only implied, by an input written as x: add/sum ("take output sum of step add"). In the DAG it is explicit: steps come in an order in which every step comes after the steps it depends on, and each input says whether it comes from a recipe input (workflow_input) or from another step's output (step_output). A cycle (two steps waiting for each other) cannot be put in such an order, so the Registry rejects it. [#what-the-dag-contains] shows the DAG's fields and an example.

              Who asks for the plan, and when. The Registry derives the DAG fresh on every request and never caches it, so every answer reflects the recipe as it is stored at that moment.

              # Who When Asks the Registry Uses the answer to
              1 A recipe author, in the editor Clicks Validate POST /recipes/validate (the draft text, not stored) Show whether the draft is valid, its inputs and its step order; only then enable Save

              C.5 The recipe format: a CWL profile

              A recipe is a CWL v1.2 Workflow whose steps each run an inline class: Operation: CWL's own abstract, non-executable step. The Operation's id is the OGC API Process identifier. We chose CWL over Arazzo and a custom JSON format because it is a mature, externally governed standard for chaining discrete steps.

              OGC's Best Practice for Earth Observation Application Package (OGC 20-089)^0 also packages CWL workflows for OGC API Processes, but differently. It uses CommandLineTool steps with container images that are deployed to a platform (Processes Part 2). Our recipes use Operation steps that call processes that already run elsewhere.

              C.5.1 Standard CWL we use

              CWL construct Used for
              class: Workflow, cwlVersion: v1.2 The recipe; id, label, doc seed the catalogue id, title and description on first load
              inputs Parameters (float, int, string, enum, arrays, Any, optional, default) and data inputs (File / File[] + format)
              outputs + outputSource What the recipe returns
              steps with run: {class: Operation, id: …} One step = one OGC API Process
              step label and the Operation's own label Two different names: what the step does in this recipe, and the app that does it (see "Naming a step and its app" below)
              step in: / out: Wiring: short form (x: add/sum) or long form (source: + valueFrom:); output of process a becomes input of process b
              format (IANA media types via $namespaces) Media type of a data input; also used non-standardly on non-File process inputs and outputs (see below)
              valueFrom (four patterns) Reshaping a value for a specific process
              requirements StepInputExpressionRequirement and InlineJavascriptRequirement, declared where valueFrom is used

              The Registry reads CWL with a purpose-built parser, not a general CWL engine. It treats unsupported features in two ways:

              • Rejected with an error: a cwlVersion that is not v1.x (any v1.x is accepted, not only v1.2), non-Workflow documents, non-Operation steps (CommandLineTool, ExpressionTool), list-form steps: or in:, cycles, unknown step references, unsupported valueFrom expressions, and the retired x- extension names.
              • Silently ignored: scatter, when and $import/$include. A recipe using when passes Validate, and the step then runs every time.

              CWL expressions are JavaScript; we never execute them. Four valueFrom forms are recognised by pattern-matching: a field path, a map over a list picking a field, a map building an object, and an object template.

              requirements: declared for CWL standard, ignored by our stack.

              CWL requires a recipe to declare the features it uses. A step input with valueFrom needs StepInputExpressionRequirement, and an expression that is real JavaScript, such as the map lines in the roofer recipe, also needs InlineJavascriptRequirement; a plain field path such as $(self.model_3d_id) does not.

              Neither the Registry nor the Cook reads these declarations: a recipe without them validates and runs the same in our stack. We declare them anyway, so that our recipes stay standard CWL: without them, cwltool --validate rejects a recipe that uses valueFrom. The Registry does not check for them either, another case of finding 7. Of the bundled recipes, only playground-1 (roofer) needs them ([#appendix-c]).

              The Registry checks structure and wiring, not types: it checks that every connection points at something that exists, but not that the value sent fits what the other end expects. The reference validator, cwltool --validate, does check this, and it caught a real mistake the Registry had accepted during the first Plugfest. In the roofer recipe (playground-1), the recipe input formats was declared as a list of fixed choices (a CWL enum: gpkg or cityjson), but the convert step declared the process input it feeds as a list of free text (string[]). For CWL those are different types, so the connection between them is invalid, even though roofer itself would have accepted the values. We fixed the step's declaration to the same list of fixed choices.

              Naming a step and its app. CWL gives every step two places for a name, and we use them for two different things:

              Where in the CWL Example Means Shown in the UI as
              The step's key and its label multiply: with label: "Step 2 — Multiply" What this step does in the recipe The title of the step's card, and the second name on its graph node
              The Operation's label (under run) label: "Multiply two numbers" The app (OGC API Process) that does it The app's name, everywhere: graphs, cards, the cost summary
              The Operation's id id: multiply-two-numbers The process identifier the Cook calls Only where a technical id is useful

              The split matters because a user may swap the app in a step ([#extensions-we-needed]). The step stays the same ("Step 2 — Multiply"), but the app changes ("Multiply three numbers"): the Registry rewrites the step's Operation, and with it its label. An early version of the UI showed whatever name was nearest at hand, so one step appeared as its key on one screen, its app on another, and the title from the remote process description on a third. Now the Registry passes the Operation's label to every client (process_label in the DAG), and every screen follows one rule:

              • App name: the Operation's label in the recipe; else the name of our own tool; else the title in the app's OGC API Process description; else its process id.
              • Step name: the step's label; else its key.

              A recipe author is therefore in control of every name a user sees. A step without a label shows its raw key (for example varidian-cook_subsidence-analysis), so we recommend giving every step a short label.

              format beyond File inputs. CWL defines format only for File parameters. Our Cook also reads it on an Operation's non-File inputs and outputs: an input is sent as an OGC qualified value ({"value": …, "mediaType": …}), and an output is requested in that media type. In playground-2, Buffer's GML output is passed to GetArea this way. Strict CWL ignores these with a warning, so this use only means something to our Cook.

              C.5.2 Extensions we needed

              CWL describes what to run, not where. Three extension fields fill the gaps a recipe has in an App Store. They live in a namespace each recipe declares:

              $namespaces:
  nldt: https://fi-group.github.io/nldt-tb2/ns#
              Field Where Why it is needed
              nldt:processLink (rel, type, href, internalHref) On a step's run Binds an abstract Operation to a concrete OGC API Process. The Cook calls internalHref; href is for people reading the recipe
              nldt:compatibleTools Top level, {stepId: [toolId, …]} The recipe owner's allowlist of alternative tools a hub may swap in for a step

              Two addresses in nldt:processLink: href and internalHref. A process link carries two addresses for the same process:

              • href is the public address, the one anyone can open in a browser. For our own tools it is under https://nldt-tb2.clearly.app/api/tools/…; for an external process it is that server's own address.
              • internalHref is the address the Cook calls. For an external process it is the same public address. For our own tools it is their address on the stack's private Docker network (for example http://tool-add:8000/processes/add-two-numbers), which works only from inside that network.

              The second address exists because the Cook runs on the same private network as our own tools. Calling them there is direct: the request does not leave the server, and does not depend on the public gateway, its TLS certificate or the Cloudflare proxy in front of it. It is not a development shortcut; the deployed Cook uses it on every run.

              Still, it is a deployment detail that has leaked into the recipe. A recipe should say which process a step uses, not how one particular installation reaches it: http://tool-add:8000 means nothing to anyone reading the recipe, or to another cook running the same recipe elsewhere. A cleaner design keeps only the public href in the recipe and lets each cook map known public addresses to faster internal ones in its own configuration, the way it already does for data (LOCATION_REWRITES, [#data-inputs-vs-parameters]). We recommend that change ([#recommendations-and-future-work]).

              From x- to nldt:. Our first version used OpenAPI-style names: x-process-link, x-compatible-tools, x-json-schema. CWL allows extension fields only with a prefix declared in $namespaces, and cwltool --validate rejected every bundled recipe because it read the x- fields' contents as CWL. After renaming to a declared nldt: namespace, all five bundled recipes pass the reference validator, cwltool --validate (cwltool 3.3.20260925135507), and the derived schemas and DAGs are unchanged.

              Two harmless warnings remain: cwltool still resolves type keys inside our fields, and reads roofer's process ids (roofer:validate_point_cloud:v1) as an undeclared prefix. The Registry now rejects the old x- names with an error naming the replacement. The namespace IRI does not resolve yet (404), which CWL does not require.

              Running a recipe vs swapping tools. Running a recipe as written needs no central process registry: each step carries its own nldt:processLink. Tool swaps are more limited:

              • Alternatives are checked against a built-in catalogue of our four tools (add, multiply, multiply3, buffer). Unknown ids in the allowlist are silently dropped, so a hub cannot swap in an external process such as roofer or ZOO-Project.
              • A step without an allowlist entry allows only its own tool.
              • A swap remaps inputs by position: the new tool's first input gets the old tool's first input's wiring, and so on. Inputs the new tool adds become new workflow inputs. This works for multiply → multiply3, but two tools whose inputs share an order but not a meaning would be wired wrongly without any error.
              • The catalogue exists in four copies (Registry, Cook fallback, and two in the UI) that must stay in sync.
              • Hand-editing nldt:processLink can point a step at any process; only the Wizard's swaps are allowlisted.

              C.5.3 Data inputs vs parameters

              A recipe declares a data input with standard CWL, not an extension: type: File (or File[]) plus a format. Every other input is a parameter.

              • The value is always a reference: {"class": "File", "location": "https://…", "format": "…/application/geo+json"}.
              • The Cook passes it on as an OGC by-reference input, {"href": …, "type": …}. The process fetches the data itself.
              • Where a process expects its own shape, the recipe adapts the File with valueFrom on that step. Roofer, for example, wants [{"kind": "url", "url": …}].
              • The Registry lists data inputs as data_inputs in the DAG. The Wizard builds its Data Ingredients step from that, so any recipe, including one just edited, shows its data needs.

              Location rewriting. A data input's location is the public Hub Datasets address the browser picked, under https://nldt-tb2.clearly.app/api/hub/…. For our own tools only, the Cook swaps that public prefix for Hub Datasets' address on the private network (LOCATION_REWRITES), for the same reason as internalHref ([#extensions-we-needed]). External processes always receive the public address, and fetch the data from there; this only works because Hub Datasets is publicly reachable.

              We considered and rejected two alternatives. A new x-data-ingredient field would duplicate a concept CWL already has. Inferring data inputs from the process description is impossible: roofer describes its point-cloud input as a LAS/LAZ URL only in prose.

              C.5.4 Parameters: CWL types vs JSON Schema

              OGC API Processes describe every input in JSON Schema; CWL has its own, weaker type system. For most inputs the CWL type is enough and the Registry derives the JSON Schema itself.

              Process input (JSON Schema) Recipe input (CWL) nldt:jsonSchema needed?
              {type: number} float No
              {type: string, enum: [gpkg, cityjson]} enum No
              A GeoJSON or LAZ file File + format No
              {oneOf: [...]} with const, pattern, minItems (roofer's bag) Only Any Yes

              nldt:jsonSchema is descriptive only: the client builds its form from it, the Cook does not validate against it. Its weakness is that it is a copy pasted from the process description, so it goes stale if the process changes. Referencing the process's own schema would be better.

              The process description fills the gaps, at display time. Most recipes say little about their parameters: a CWL type and perhaps a label. The app behind each step usually says much more, in a standard place: an OGC API Process description gives every input a title, a description and a JSON Schema, and JSON Schema has standard keywords for a default and for limits such as minimum. Varidian's subsidence process, for example, publishes gw with default -1.0, thickness with default 1.5 and minimum 0.1, and threshold with default 0.25.

              So the Wizard fetches the description of each step's process (once per session, through the Registry's fetch-description proxy for external servers) and, for each recipe parameter, reads the input it is wired to. Two rules keep this safe:

              • The recipe always wins. A label, description or default written in the CWL is used as written. The process only fills what the recipe leaves out.
              • Nothing is written back. The process's information is shown, and its defaults are sent when a field is left empty, but the stored recipe never changes. If the process changes its description, the next Wizard session shows the new one, so this cannot go stale the way a pasted nldt:jsonSchema can.

              This works for any recipe and any process, not only the ones in this testbed. Its limit is what process providers publish: the subsidence process gives no default or example for its GeoJSON input, so that field stays required, and the form can only show the expected shape (an empty FeatureCollection). A JSON Schema example entry would let the form show a real polygon ([#collaboration]).

              C.6 The Cookbook Registry

              The Cookbook Registry is the tender's cookbook: it stores each recipe's raw CWL and derives, on every request, its input JSON Schema and execution DAG.

              The tender suggests building the cookbook with OGC Building Blocks. We did not package the Registry's API as a Building Block: it is a small, purpose-built REST API and no existing block describes a recipe registry. The better candidates are the recipe format itself and the catalogue record. The nldt: CWL profile ([#the-recipe-format-a-cwl-profile]) and the recipe record ([#catalogue-and-recipe-metadata]) each have a schema and examples, which is what a Building Block contains. Publishing them through the OGC Building Blocks register is future work ([#recommendations-and-future-work]).

              Endpoint Purpose
              GET /recipes List recipes (id, title, description, keywords, timestamps)
              GET /recipes/{id} The CWL, verbatim (application/cwl+yaml)
              GET /recipes/{id}/original The bundled, known-good CWL, used by the editor's Reset
              GET /recipes/{id}/schema Derived JSON Schema of the recipe's inputs
              GET /recipes/{id}/dag Derived execution graph: steps in topological order, resolved inputs, data_inputs and workflow_outputs
              POST /recipes/{id}/effective Schema and DAG with a hub's toolOverrides applied; never stored
              POST /recipes/validate Checks a CWL document without storing it; always 200 with valid: true/false
              POST /recipes Validates, stores (upsert), and publishes a catalogue record
              DELETE /recipes/{id} Removes the recipe and retracts its record
              GET /processes/fetch-description?url=… Fetches any OGC API Process description server-side for the guided builder, avoiding browser CORS limits. No allowlist or SSRF protection

              Recipes are stored in SQLite in a Docker volume. Schema and DAG are derived fresh on every request, never cached.

              CWL vs DAG. CWL is a document written for people: order in the file is arbitrary and it carries labels, docs and extensions. The DAG is the machine form: steps in topological order, each input resolved to a workflow input or another step's output. [#design-principle] explains why every part of the stack works from the DAG.

              C.6.2 Design choices

              Two design choices matter:

              • Validation is a gate, twice. The editor's Validate calls the validate endpoint; Save re-validates server-side. Nothing is stored unless a DAG can be built. Validation is structural only: it never calls the processes.

              • Catalogue outages don't lose recipes. A recipe is stored first, then published. If the catalogue is down, the response says catalog_published: false plus the error, rather than failing.

              One weakness is that if a stored recipe no longer parses (for example an old one still using x- names), /schema and /dag return HTTP 500 because the parse error isn't caught. Re-seeding after a redeploy replaces stored recipes.

              C.7 The Cook Orchestrator

              The Cook is itself an OGC API Processes Part 1: Core 1.0 service with exactly one process, cook, so any OGC API Processes client can execute a recipe without knowing anything about CWL. It is non-agentic: it walks the DAG and decides nothing.

              The cook process takes recipeId, parameters and toolOverrides ({stepId: toolId}) and returns result. It declares the conformance classes core, json, oas30, job-list and dismiss.

              C.7.1 What happens when you click 'Execute'

              In short, the browser hands the recipe to the Cook and gets a job number back at once. The Cook then works through the recipe's DAG on its own: it starts every step whose inputs are ready, calls that step's OGC API Process, waits for it (following the process's own job when the process runs asynchronously), and passes its outputs on to the steps that need them. Meanwhile the browser asks the Cook every 2 seconds how far it is.

              In detail:

              1. In the browser (Status tab). Execute collects three things:

              • Parameters: each value entered in the Wizard; for an empty field, its default (the recipe's own, else the app's, see [#parameters-cwl-types-vs-json-schema]).
              • Data ingredients: each dataset picked in the Wizard, as a CWL File, {"class": "File", "location": "<URL of the dataset>", "format": "<media type>"}. The location is a link; the data itself is never sent.
              • App swaps: toolOverrides, {stepId: toolId}, if the hub swapped an app for this run.

              It posts these to the Cook, POST /processes/cook/execution with the header Prefer: respond-async and the body {"inputs": {"recipeId": …, "parameters": {…}, "toolOverrides": {…}}}. The Cook answers immediately with 201 Created, a Location header with the job's address (https://nldt-tb2.clearly.app/api/orchestrator/jobs/{jobID}) and the job's first status (accepted). Execute is disabled while a required parameter or data ingredient is still missing.

              2. In the Cook: get the plan. The job runs on its own background thread, so the Cook can take other requests meanwhile. It asks the Registry for the recipe's DAG (GET /recipes/{id}/dag), or, if the hub swapped an app, for the effective DAG (POST /recipes/{id}/effective).

              3. In the Cook: start every step that is ready. A step is ready when every step it takes input from has finished. Ready steps start at once, each on its own worker thread, at most 4 at a time (COOK_MAX_PARALLEL). Steps that do not depend on each other therefore run in parallel; a chain still runs one step after another. In playground-2, for example, Hello World (Python), Hello World (JavaScript), Centroid and Buffer all start together, and GetArea starts the moment Buffer has finished.

              4. In the Cook: prepare one step's request. For each input of the step's process, the DAG says where its value comes from:

              • From a recipe input: the value the browser sent; if none, the recipe's CWL default; if none and the input is optional, it is left out; otherwise the run fails with "Missing required parameter".
              • From another step's output: the value that step returned.
              • Reshaped, if the recipe's CWL has a valueFrom for it: a field picked out of the value, or a new object built from it. This is how a recipe hands data to a process in that process's own shape, for example roofer's [{"kind": "url", "url": …}].
              • Data inputs (CWL Files) become OGC by-reference inputs, {"href": "<location>", "type": "<media type>"}: the process downloads the data itself. For our own tools on the internal Docker network, the Cook first swaps the public address for the internal one (LOCATION_REWRITES).
              • A format on a non-File input wraps the value as an OGC qualified value, {"value": …, "mediaType": …}. A format on an output asks the process for that output in that media type.

              5. In the Cook: call the process. The Cook posts {"inputs": {…}} to the step's process, at its nldt:processLink.internalHref plus /execution, again with Prefer: respond-async. A process can answer in one of two ways, and the Cook handles both:

              • Synchronously: 200 OK with the results in the response. Our own four tools always do this; they ignore the header.
              • Asynchronously: 201 or 202 with a job of its own. Roofer, the Geolabs ZOO-Project processes and Varidian's subsidence process support this. The Cook finds the job's address (the Location header, else the job's self or monitor link, else /jobs/{jobID}), then asks it for its status every 2 seconds, copying the remote status, progress and message into the step's own progress. When the job reports successful, the Cook fetches …/results. When it reports failed, the Cook fetches the error document from the same address, because some servers (ZOO-Project) leave their status message at "accepted" even after failing.

              6. In the Cook: read the outputs. Processes return results in different shapes: our own tools as {"outputs": {…}}, standard OGC results as {name: value} or {name: {"value": …}}, ZOO-Project as multipart text when a process has several outputs, and some servers as the bare value of a single-output process. The Cook turns each of these into one shape, {outputName: value}, and stores it. Every step waiting for this step's outputs may now be ready (back to point 3). The Cook also records a trace entry: exactly what the step was sent, what it returned, and whether its process was found through its nldt:processLink or the fallback table ([#where-a-step-runs]).

              7. In the Cook: finish, or stop on the first failure. When all steps are done, the Cook picks out the recipe's outputs (workflow_outputs) and marks the job successful. If a step fails, the Cook starts no new steps, stops the ones still running, dismisses their remote jobs, and marks the job failed, saying where the failure came from ([#where-a-failure-came-from]). Time limits apply to each HTTP call, each remote job and the whole run ([#sync-and-async]).

              8. Back in the browser. While the Cook works, the browser asks GET /jobs/{jobID} every 2 seconds. The answer has the overall status, a progress from 0 to 100 (finished steps count fully, a running step by its remote job's own progress if it reports one), a one-line message such as "Step 2 of 3: Reconstruct buildings — running", and the status of every step. The Status tab shows this as a list of steps. A Cancel button sends DELETE /jobs/{jobID}; the Cook stops between steps, or within 2 seconds while waiting on a remote job, and dismisses that job too.

              When the job is successful, the browser fetches GET /jobs/{jobID}/results: the recipe's outputs plus the trace. It shows each output (a GeoJSON output as a summary, since it may be large) and immediately saves the result to the Sandbox Stack Recipe Results dataset in Hub Datasets, named after the recipe with the date and time. If any output is GeoJSON, that output is saved, so it can be shown on a map; otherwise the whole result is saved as JSON. [#status-tab-and-saved-results] describes where saved results can be viewed.

              The Cook is non-agentic throughout: it never decides which process to call or how to combine results. Everything it does follows from the DAG.

              C.7.2 Sync and async

              There are two layers. The outer layer is how the caller experiences waiting: sync (hold the connection) or async (Prefer: respond-async, get a job, poll /jobs/{id}). The inner layer is how the Cook calls each step: it always asks for asynchronous execution (Prefer: respond-async). A process that answers synchronously anyway is used as-is; one that returns a job is polled. Our own four tools always answer synchronously.

              Sync is the protocol default (a caller that sends no Prefer header) and returns {result, trace} in one response; the App Store UI always uses async ([#what-happens-when-you-click-execute]). Async returns 201 with a Location header; job status includes per-step progress, a 0–100 progress and a message. Each asynchronous cook job runs on its own background thread, so the Cook accepts new jobs while others are running. The job store is in memory, so a restart loses jobs.

              Async is mandatory in practice. Roofer's building reconstruction outlives any HTTP or proxy timeout. Remote jobs are polled every 2 s, and the time limits are configurable:

              Setting Applies to Default In docker-compose
              TOOL_REQUEST_TIMEOUT Each HTTP call 10 s 90 s
              TOOL_JOB_TIMEOUT Each remote job 300 s —
              TOOL_JOB_POLL_INTERVAL Polling a remote job 2 s —
              COOK_TIMEOUT The whole run 900 s —
              COOK_MAX_PARALLEL Steps running at once 4 —

              When a job times out or is cancelled, the remote job is dismissed (best-effort DELETE /jobs/{id}). Dismissing a cook job stops it between steps, or within one poll interval while it waits on a remote job.

              C.7.3 Where a step runs

              A step names its process by an abstract id. The Cook resolves it to a URL in this order: the step's own nldt:processLink.internalHref, then a static fallback table (TOOL_REGISTRY) for the four built-in tools. href is never called; [#extensions-we-needed] explains why there are two addresses, and why we would drop the internal one. The fallback keeps older recipes working; there is no dynamic discovery service.

              We tested the vendor-agnostic claim through the real UI in an early build (early September): replacing the multiply step's process with a divide process, without code changes, returned (2 + 3) / 4 = 1.25, with the trace showing the new process was called. The divide tool was later replaced by multiply3; today the Wizard offers the equivalent swap, multiply → multiply3, on recipe-add-multiply-demo.

              C.7.4 Where a failure came from

              A recipe calls processes the user does not control, so a failed run must say whose failure it is. A failed cook job's status carries an errorSource field, and so does each failed step. It has one of three values:

              • tool: the step's OGC API Process returned an error status, its job failed or timed out, it could not be reached, or its response could not be read. The field also names the process id, its URL and the step.
              • registry: the Cookbook Registry could not be reached or did not know the recipe.
              • cook: the Cook itself: a missing parameter, the overall time limit, a configuration problem or a bug.

              The Status tab uses this field to tell the user "Error source: external tool buffer-geometry during step 'Buffer'" or "Error source: Cook Orchestrator (this platform)". Failed steps in the step list start with "Tool error:" or "Cook Orchestrator error:".

              Two cases can be labelled wrongly. A tool response the Cook cannot parse counts as a tool error, even if the Cook's parsing is at fault. A network problem between the Cook and a tool also counts as a tool error, although the message says "unreachable". errorSource is a field beyond OGC API Processes' statusInfo; synchronous execution still returns only the error text.

              The catalogue implements OGC API Records Part 1: Core over a real CKAN 2.11 instance. Recipe metadata in DCAT-AP-NL is not implemented; [#recipe-metadata-in-dcat-ap-nl] gives a first proposal.

              C.8.1 Records Wrapper over CKAN

              The Records Wrapper exposes the landing page, conformance, collections, items (parameters q, limit and offset only) and a single item, with one collection, recipes, mapped to one CKAN organisation. It translates each OGC request into CKAN Action API calls (package_search, package_show, …) and maps the results back to Records features.

              Records Part 1 is read-only, so publishing uses two non-standard write endpoints, POST and DELETE on /collections/{id}/items, which the Registry calls to publish and retract records.

              C.8.2 What a record contains

              A record carries discovery metadata only: title, description, keywords, timestamps, type: recipe, and a link to the CWL in the Cookbook Registry. The CWL itself lives only in the Registry: the catalogue describes the thing, but it does not hold it.

              The link uses the relation …/rel/ogc/1.0/recipe-source with media type application/cwl+yaml. That relation sits in OGC's namespace but is not registered there. A registered relation (describedby, or alternate with the CWL media type) or one in our own namespace would be correct.

              Two gaps in the records today:

              • The link's href is built from COOKBOOK_PUBLIC_URL, which was set to the Docker-internal http://cookbook-registry:8000, so an outside client could not follow it. The gateway setup now sets the public address (https://nldt-tb2.clearly.app/api/cookbook), and records carry it from the moment a recipe is stored again, as happened for the bundled recipes after the redeployment of 29 October.
              • Keywords are empty for all bundled recipes, because the seed script sets none.

              C.8.3 Recipe metadata in DCAT-AP-NL: what is missing, and what to add

              The catalogue we built carries discovery metadata only ([#what-a-record-contains]); we did not implement a DCAT-AP-NL profile for recipes. But building and running recipes end to end showed clearly what such metadata has to say. This section sets that out: what DCAT-AP-NL already offers, what a recipe needs that it does not cover, and what we propose to add.

              What DCAT-AP-NL already offers. DCAT-AP-NL is the Dutch profile of DCAT-AP, itself a profile of W3C DCAT. It describes catalogues and what is in them: datasets, their distributions (a downloadable or accessible form of the data, with a media type and an access address), and data services (an API, with an endpoint address and an endpoint description). Around these it has the usual descriptive metadata: title, description, keywords, theme, spatial and temporal coverage, publisher, contact point, licence, access rights, conformance to a standard (dct:conformsTo), version, dates, and general relations between resources.

              That covers much of a recipe's discovery metadata. A recipe has a title, a description, keywords, a theme, an area it is meant for, a publisher and a licence, and all of those fit. It also covers the recipe's definition as a file: the CWL document can be a distribution, with its media type and the standards it conforms to.

              What a recipe needs that DCAT-AP-NL does not cover. A recipe is neither data nor a running service: it is an executable plan that combines both. Everything about how it runs, what it needs and what it produces has no place in DCAT-AP-NL today. Each row below comes from something the testbed showed:

              What it produces, in which format Outputs ([#what-the-dag-contains]) No Outputs: name, description, media type
              How it runs Sync and async, duration ([#sync-and-async], finding 12) No Expected execution mode (synchronous or job-based) and typical duration

              Proposal. Extend DCAT-AP-NL with a small recipe module rather than bending datasets or data services to fit:

              • A class Recipe (subclass of dcat:Resource) with the normal descriptive properties, plus links to its steps, data inputs, parameters and outputs, its expected execution mode, and its access and cost requirements.
              • A Step names its role in the recipe and the data service (OGC API Process) it calls, plus any allowed alternatives. Each data service is described once, as DCAT-AP-NL already allows, with its endpoint and process description.
              • A Data input and an Output each carry a name and the media types they accept or produce. DPROD's input and output ports are a candidate to reuse for these.
              • A Parameter points at the app's own input schema instead of copying it.
              • The CWL file is the recipe's distribution, conforming to CWL v1.2 and to the recipe profile.
              • Results produced by a recipe run carry provenance back to the recipe.

              Most of this metadata can be generated from the recipe itself: the Registry already derives the steps, their apps, the data inputs with media types, the parameters and the outputs ([#what-the-dag-contains]), and the apps' process descriptions provide titles, descriptions and defaults ([#parameters-cwl-types-vs-json-schema]). Only discovery facets (theme, target roles, phase), access, cost and ownership need to be written by people. That makes a recipe record cheap to keep accurate.

              In CKAN, the extra fields would come from ckanext-scheming and the RDF output from ckanext-dcat. Neither is installed today; CKAN runs only the envvars and datastore plugins.

              C.9 The client: App Store UI

              The App Store UI finds recipes, binds them to a hub's own data and tools, and executes them. It is a static, no-build web app published to GitHub Pages and calls the services directly from the browser. Its aim is that someone who has never seen a CWL file or an OGC API Process description can still run a recipe and understand what it does.

              Built in the image of Clearly Hub. The App Store UI follows the structure, styling and overall look and feel of Future Insight's own product, Clearly Hub, also called the Open Urban Platform (OUP). The idea behind the OUP is a shared platform where organisations, each with its own hub (a municipality, for example), publish and combine data, apps and digital twins, and work on them together. The App Store's navigation (Home, Hubs, Recipes, Digital Twin, Data, Apps, Connect), its "active hub", its catalogue cards and its recipe pages all come from there.

              We chose this deliberately. It shows recipes in the setting where they would actually be used, next to a hub's own data and apps, rather than in a bare test client. Parts of the OUP that are outside this research topic (reviews, hub management, digital twins, pricing) are shown as they would look, but are not functional in the sandbox.

              C.9.1 How the UI uses the recipe

              The UI never interprets CWL itself ([#design-principle]). Everything it shows about a recipe comes from the Registry's answer (DAG and input schema), from the catalogue record, or from the OGC API Process descriptions of the recipe's apps:

              An app's name The CWL Operation label (process_label in the DAG), with fallbacks ([#standard-cwl-we-use])

              C.9.2 Catalogue, recipe pages and home page

              Catalogue. Recipes from the Records Wrapper, filterable by facets. Separate pages list Data (Hub Datasets) and Apps (live OGC API Processes, from each one's own process description).

              Recipe detail. Metadata, the recipe's graph, and its compatible data and apps. A banner on a live recipe points to the Recipe Wizard tab, where a run is set up.

              Home page. Recent activity: the recipes, apps and datasets live in the sandbox.

              C.9.3 The Recipe Wizard

              The Wizard binds a recipe to a hub in four steps. Every step shows the same recipe graph above its own controls, so the user always sees the whole recipe while setting up one part of it:

              • Inputs: each data input (marked "data · GeoJSON" and so on) and each parameter, by its readable title.
              • Apps: the app of each step, named as in [#standard-cwl-we-use], with the step's name beside it.
              • Results: the recipe's outputs, always in their own column at the end, even when a step's output is also used by a later step.
              • Saved to: where the result is stored.

              Colours show the state of the setup: green for ready (a dataset picked, a value or default set), red for still needed, amber for an app swapped for this run. The graph is drawn from the effective DAG ([#design-principle]), so it always shows what the Cook will actually run.

              1. Tool & Engine. One card per step: the step's name, and the app that runs it. If the recipe owner allows alternatives (nldt:compatibleTools), "Change" offers them. Swapping an app asks the Registry for the effective recipe and redraws the graph straight away: swapping multiply for multiply3 on recipe-add-multiply-demo turns that app amber and adds its third input, z, to the graph and to the Parameters step. The swap applies to this run only; the stored recipe never changes.
              2. Data Ingredients. For each File input, a dataset from Hub Datasets whose media type matches the input's format.
              3. Parameters. The parameters are grouped by the app that reads them, so the form reads as "this app needs these settings" rather than as CWL wiring.

              Each group starts with the same app row as step 1, and a "Process description" link to the app's OGC API Process description (JSON).

              Each field shows the parameter's readable title, its technical name as a small tag, a chip ("Default -1", "Required" or "Optional"), one line of help (its description and limits, such as "Must be at least 0.1"), and a "Definition" link to the same process description.

              A default is shown as grey placeholder text: an untouched field sends it, typing replaces it, clearing the field brings it back. Structured values (objects, lists, GeoJSON) are typed as JSON in the same way, with the expected shape as placeholder; there are no special template buttons. [#parameters-cwl-types-vs-json-schema] explains where titles, descriptions and defaults come from. A parameter used by two apps appears once, under the first, with "Also used by …"; one no app reads appears under "Not used by any app".

              1. Output. Where the result is saved. In this testbed every result is saved to the Sandbox Stack Recipe Results dataset in the nLDT Sandbox Hub, so the hub and dataset dropdowns are shown, as they would be in a full App Store, but locked to that destination, with a note saying why.

              Every step can be opened at any time; nothing forces completing one step before the next. A setup summary beside the steps, styled as a basket, lists the apps used and an execution fee for each. The fees are illustrative and nothing is charged; it only shows where pricing would appear (finding 25).

              C.9.4 Status tab and saved results

              Status. Shows the recipe graph, the data ingredients, and the parameters grouped per app exactly as in the Wizard, read-only, each with the value that will be sent (marked "default" when it is one). It also works when the Wizard was never opened: missing values fall back to their defaults, the same way the Wizard does. Execute stays disabled, with a warning naming what is missing, until every required parameter and data ingredient has a value. Execute, progress, cancelling, the result and saving it are described in [#what-happens-when-you-click-execute]; a failure says whose it is ([#where-a-failure-came-from]).

              Saved results. Every successful run is saved to the Sandbox Stack Recipe Results dataset. Its Preview, on the Data page, shows all saved results on one map, each as a layer that can be switched on and off. The layer list shows each result by its recipe's name, with the date and time it was saved. A zoom button per result fits the map to that result and opens its label; a result with no geometry (for example the number from recipe-add-multiply-demo) says so instead.

              A result that links to a CityJSON model, such as roofer's reconstruction (artifacts.cityjson.href), is shown by its building footprints: the browser fetches the model, takes each building's LoD0 footprint, converts it from RD New (EPSG:28992) to WGS84, and draws it; clicking a building shows its id and its roof and ground heights. Only the footprints are drawn, since the map is 2D. "Delete selected" removes only the results whose layer is switched on, after a confirmation that lists each of them. Hub Datasets delete them one by one (DELETE /datasets/{id}/resources/{resourceId}); built-in datasets such as the live PDOK boundary cannot be deleted.

              C.9.5 Orchestrator editor

              The recipe's CWL as syntax-highlighted text, as a diagram and as a guided builder. The builder adds a process by URL: it reads the process description and generates the step, its inputs and outputs, and its process link. It connects inputs and marks an input as data or parameter. It displays valueFrom reshaping but does not write it; that is still done by hand. Save is enabled only after a successful Validate, editing again disables it, and Save re-validates on the server. Reset reloads the bundled original.

              The guided builder answers the main usability finding from the first plugfest: writing a recipe required knowing exactly how we had structured the CWL.

              C.10 Plugfest and interoperability

              At the first plugfest, we executed recipes against external processes from other participants. They ran end to end after small fixes.

              What interoperated:

              • Roofer staging (validate → reconstruct → convert), called by reference with a 1.2 MB crop of AHN3 tile c_37en2 (Delft), from TU Delft's 3dfier example data. Roofer staging returns fixture results and does not download the point cloud, so this proves the plumbing, not a reconstruction: whatever the input, it returns the same sample model, 165 buildings in Nijmegen.
              • Geolabs ZOO-Project processes (Buffer, GetArea, Centroid), on the ZOO-Project server GeoLabs runs on Geonovum's testbed host, fetching a polygon by reference from a public URL.
              • OGC API Processes clients that build URLs themselves can call our cook directly, since it is itself a process. Clients that follow the links in its answers got wrong addresses behind the gateway until this was fixed (see Conformance below).

              What we learned from participants:

              • Writing a recipe was not user-friendly: users had to know how we had structured the CWL. This led to the guided builder ([#the-client-app-store-ui]).
              • A participant asked to parameterise the buffer recipe by city name instead of always using Rotterdam's outline. This is future work ([#recommendations-and-future-work]).
              • A participant asked whether a client can read a process's inputs automatically when adding it as a step. The guided builder now does this from the process description.
              • By-reference data must be reachable from the process, not just the browser. An external process can only fetch data at a public address, which is why Hub Datasets is published.

They expect the server to host OGC's test process, echo, which accepts every kind of input (text, numbers, objects, files by reference) and returns it unchanged. Our processes take specific inputs: two numbers, a geometry and a distance, or a recipe id. So the suite's test values are rejected, as they should be (the Cook, for example, is asked to run the recipe "teststring"), and these tests cannot pass. They test the server's execution machinery with a process it does not have, not whether our processes behave correctly. Hosting a small echo process next to the Cook would let the suite test the Cook's job handling end to end ([#recommendations-and-future-work]).

              C.11 Findings

              Twenty-five findings, grouped by the standard or theme they concern.

              C.11.1 Recipe format (CWL)

              1. CWL works as a recipe format, but not unextended. It cannot say which OGC API Process a step is (nldt:processLink) or which alternatives a hub may choose (nldt:compatibleTools).
              2. CWL's Operation class fits remote processes. An abstract step maps one-to-one onto an OGC API Process, with no CommandLineTool involved. CWL's standard requirements (for example DockerRequirement and ResourceRequirement) describe running a tool locally; none applies to an Operation that calls a remote process.
              3. Data inputs need no extension. File + format separates ingredients from parameters and maps cleanly onto OGC href inputs.
              4. CWL and OGC API Processes use different type systems. CWL is enough for a recipe's structure, not for describing process inputs as precisely as JSON Schema. A CWL-based format needs a bridge (nldt:jsonSchema), ideally a reference rather than a copy.
              5. CWL expressions are JavaScript. A lightweight cook either embeds a JavaScript engine or supports a pattern-matched subset of valueFrom, as ours does.
              6. CWL's extension rule is enforced by tooling. Extension fields need a namespace prefix declared in $namespaces. Our first, OpenAPI-style x- names made every recipe invalid for cwltool --validate; with a declared nldt: namespace, all pass.
              7. A purpose-built cook checks less than a CWL validator. cwltool caught a real type error our Registry had accepted (an enum list wired into a string[] input in the roofer recipe). Our reader validates structure and wiring, not types; running the reference validator alongside is cheap.
              8. CWL has no field for media types of non-File values. format is only defined for Files, so requesting the media type of a non-File process input or output has no standard CWL field; our use of format there only means something to our Cook.
              9. One reader of the recipe avoids drift. Only the Registry parses CWL; everything else asks it for the DAG and schema ([#design-principle]). The tool catalogue was not centralised, and now exists in four copies ([#extensions-we-needed]).
              10. A step and its app need separate names. CWL already gives a step two labels: the step's own, for what it does in the recipe, and its Operation's, for the app that does it. Showing them consistently ([#standard-cwl-we-use]) matters once apps can be swapped: the step stays, the app changes. Without one rule, a user sees three different names for the same thing.

              C.11.2 OGC API Processes

              1. Process descriptions don't say which inputs are datasets. Roofer describes its point-cloud input only in prose, so a platform cannot infer data ingredients; the recipe has to declare them.
              2. Async execution is mandatory in practice. Long processes outlive HTTP and proxy timeouts, so the Cook always requests async and polls.
              3. Media types are a gap for 3D and point-cloud data. LAZ has no IANA-registered media type; application/vnd.laszip is a convention.
              4. Version. We implement Part 1: Core 1.0 and chain through CWL, not Part 3 (Workflows) nested execution.
              5. A recipe can itself be a process. Wrapping the whole Cook as one OGC API Process (cook) lets any Processes client run any recipe without knowing CWL.
              6. By-reference data must be reachable from the process, not the browser. An address that only the user's own machine or a private network can reach is useless to an external process. The Cook rewrites URLs only for its own tools.
              7. Process descriptions already carry what a parameter form needs. Titles, descriptions, defaults and limits are standard fields of an OGC API Process description and its JSON Schema. A client can read them at display time and fill a recipe's parameter form with them, without copying them into the recipe ([#parameters-cwl-types-vs-json-schema]).
              8. They rarely carry an example for structured inputs. A default works for a number; a GeoJSON area has no sensible default. JSON Schema's examples keyword exists for this, but the processes we used do not provide it, so a user still has to know the shape of the value.

              C.11.3 Catalogue and metadata

              1. Catalogue and recipe metadata are not yet aligned. OGC API Records carries discovery metadata well; DCAT-AP-NL has no recipe concept.
              2. There is no standard link relation for "this record's executable recipe". A relation could be proposed to OGC.

              C.11.4 App Store operation

              1. Editing is shared and unversioned. Saving a recipe overwrites it for everyone. There is no versioning, ownership or authentication: fine for a sandbox, not for an App Store.
              2. Swapping tools needs shared knowledge of each alternative's inputs and outputs. Ours is a built-in catalogue, so swaps are limited to our own four tools. Inputs are matched by position, not by meaning.

              C.11.5 Access, delegation and cost

              These three were outside the scope of this research, but became clear while building it, and they decide whether a recipe App Store can work in practice.

              1. Apps behind a login need delegated access, and we did not research it. Every app in this testbed is open: anyone may call it, without an account, key or token. Real apps, commercial or governmental, usually sit behind a secure gateway and need credentials. In a recipe, it is not the user who calls an app but the Cook, on the user's behalf. So the Cook would need to receive, hold and pass on access for each app in the recipe: a token for the user, valid for that app, for the duration of the run (which may be long, [#sync-and-async]), without the Cook ever seeing the user's own credentials. General-purpose mechanisms for delegation exist (for example OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, RFC 8693), but we know of no agreed way to apply them across a chain of OGC API Processes run by a third-party cook. OGC API Processes leaves authentication to each deployment.
              2. Cooks are only interchangeable once access can be delegated. The recipe architecture separates the recipe (the cookbook) from whoever runs it (the cook), so that in principle any cook can run any recipe, as our cook process allows. With authenticated apps, choosing a cook means handing it access to every app in the recipe, and a cook that calls another cook (as Varidian's process is, [#use-cases-and-bundled-recipes]) would have to pass that access on again. Until there is a trusted, standard way to delegate access to a cook, a real deployment would effectively be tied to one cook it trusts. We see this as the main blocker between this prototype and real-life use.
              3. Commercial apps need a price, and a way to pay. An App Store implies that some apps cost money. A recipe combines apps from several providers, so a run may have several prices: per run, per amount of data, or by subscription. Who pays whom, when the price is known (before the run, or only after), and how a cook that calls a paid app on the user's behalf is billed, are all open. The Wizard's setup summary, a basket with an execution fee per app ([#the-recipe-wizard]), only gives a feeling of this; the fees are illustrative and nothing is charged.

              C.11.6 Questions raised in the stand-ups

              • Recipe vs workflow (2 Oct). In our build, a recipe is a workflow (the CWL) plus its catalogue record plus its bindings to a hub's data and tools.
              • Input semantics for chaining (16 Oct). See finding 11 (process descriptions don't mark datasets) and finding 22 (inputs matched by position).
              • A recipe being itself a process (16 Oct). See finding 15.

              C.12 Recommendations and future work

              The recommendations below are addressed to the parties who can act on them.

              To Geonovum (nLDT):

              • Define recipe metadata in an extended DCAT-AP-NL profile, building on this testbed's proposal ([#recipe-metadata-in-dcat-ap-nl]).
              • Publish the recipe profile and the recipe record as OGC Building Blocks, so other cookbooks can validate against them.
              • Agree a small shared vocabulary for recipe extensions (process link, compatible tools, input schema) so cooks and cookbooks from different vendors can exchange recipes.
              • Agree media types for 3D and point-cloud formats, starting with LAZ.
              • Research delegated access for recipes: how a hub lets a cook use authenticated apps on its behalf, for one run, without handing over its own credentials (findings 23, 24). This is the main step between a sandbox and real use, and is best done together with OGC.
              • Explore how pricing and payment would work for recipes that combine paid apps from several providers (finding 25), and how prices appear in recipe metadata ([#recipe-metadata-in-dcat-ap-nl]).

              To OGC:

              • Let process descriptions mark an input as a dataset machine-readably, not only in prose.
              • Register a link relation for "executable recipe of this record".
              • Encourage process descriptions to give structured inputs an examples entry, so clients can show users a valid value.
              • Describe how an OGC API Processes server accepts delegated access from an orchestrator calling it on a user's behalf, for example as a profile building on OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, so chained execution can work with authenticated processes.

              For our own stack:

              • Run cwltool --validate as part of the Registry's validate step, to catch type errors.
              • Publish a document at the nldt namespace IRI defining the three terms (it returns 404 today).
              • Reference process schemas instead of copying them into nldt:jsonSchema.
              • Let nldt:compatibleTools reference external processes, resolving their inputs and outputs from the process description instead of a built-in catalogue, and match inputs by meaning rather than position.
              • Drop internalHref from the recipe format: keep only the public href, and let the Cook map known public addresses to internal ones in its own configuration, as it already does for data ([#extensions-we-needed]).
              • Let the Rotterdam recipe take a municipality name and fetch its outline from PDOK.
              • Let a recipe declare its preferred execution mode and have the client honour it.
              • Add versioning, ownership and authentication, plus SSRF protection on process URLs and on the description-fetch endpoint.
              • Persist the Cook's job store.
              • Retry publishing to the catalogue when CKAN is briefly unavailable, and give the Records Wrapper more than 10 seconds for writes to CKAN: after the redeployment, some catalogue writes failed while CKAN was slow, although the recipes themselves were stored.
              • Host a small echo test process next to the Cook, so OGC's test suite can check its execution and job handling end to end ([#plugfest-and-interoperability]).
              • Serve an OpenAPI 3.0 document that declares every answer the services give (response schemas, 201, the OGC 404s), and close the gaps the Geonovum checker found: limit on the process list, the job-list filters on the Cook, job endpoints on the tools, and the Records media types on the catalogue ([#plugfest-and-interoperability]).
              • Express recipe metadata in DCAT-AP-NL in the catalogue.
              • Give every step in the bundled and playground recipes a short label, and short Operation labels (the playground-2 ones are copied sentences such as "Create a welcome message string."), since these are the names users see ([#standard-cwl-we-use]).
              • Show a JSON Schema examples value as the placeholder of a structured field, once processes provide one.

              C.15 Design decisions and turning points

              The decisions that shaped the solution, oldest first. Most are a turning point: we started one way, ran into a limitation, and changed course. The full, dated log up to 22 October is DECISIONS.md in the repository; later entries come from our working notes.

              Date (2026) Decision What came before, and what we learned
              8 Sep Build the infrastructure as the real system; only the apps a recipe calls may stay trivial. Start with (a + b) × c The infrastructure is the deliverable; the apps are interchangeable by design. A trivial recipe proves the whole chain (discovery, parameters, two steps, piping, result) before any domain complexity
              8 Sep CWL as the recipe format, rather than Arazzo or our own JSON format CWL is a mature, externally governed standard for chaining discrete steps. Our own format would have needed its own tooling and governance ([#the-recipe-format-a-cwl-profile])
              8 Sep Each step is an inline CWL Operation, one per OGC API Process; recipes are read by a small purpose-built parser, not a full CWL engine Operation is CWL's own "abstract step that calls something elsewhere". Later we learned the parser is too lenient: it missed a type error that cwltool caught, and silently ignores scatter and when (finding 7)
              8 Sep OGC API Processes Part 1: Core 1.0, with chaining done by CWL inside the Cook We first chose v2.0 with Part 3 (Workflows) for chaining. Part 3 was a draft whose conformance classes we could not verify; Part 1 is stable, and CWL already describes the chain
              8 Sep The Cook is itself an OGC API Process (cook), reading the recipe's plan fresh on every run Any OGC API Processes client can run any recipe without knowing CWL, and a saved recipe needs no redeployment. The cost: a recipe never appears as its own process with typed inputs; clients only see cook (finding 15)
              8 Sep Recipes carry their own process link (nldt:processLink); a static table of our tools is only a fallback First, the Cook looked every process up in a static table: every new app meant a code change. With the link in the recipe, we swapped multiply for a divide app through the UI without touching code: (2 + 3) / 4 = 1.25
              9 Sep Shared "playground" recipe slots for the plugfests, instead of a recipe-creation UI Every mechanism needed (edit, validate, save, run) already existed; the gap was only "not enough recipes to hand out". The price: anyone can overwrite a slot, and there is no version history (finding 21, [#use-cases-and-bundled-recipes])
              10 Sep Catalogue backend: real CKAN 2.11 behind the Records Wrapper, instead of our own SQLite store The tender's architecture names CKAN. The switch touched only the wrapper's storage module, which proved the seam. CKAN's 2.9 and 2.10 images would not start; 2.11 did
              Sep–Oct The Cook always asks each process for asynchronous execution and follows its job; independent steps run in parallel The first roofer recipe (28 Sep) failed on a 10-second synchronous timeout, and on inputs that were not plain numbers or text. Real processes outlive any HTTP timeout, so async became the default for every step (finding 12). The same attempt showed that the Cook could not send credentials: the first sign of the access problem in findings 23–24
              16 Oct In the recipe editor, the CWL text is the single source of truth: text and diagram side by side, kept in sync, with the guided builder as an optional help, and nothing wired automatically We first made the guided builder the default. But people arrive with CWL to paste, and automatic wiring connects things behind their backs. The builder itself answered the first plugfest's lesson: writing a recipe required knowing how we had structured the CWL
              Oct One public address for everything (UI and /api/* behind one gateway), and the UI also on GitHub Pages Cloudflare's proxy only forwards a fixed list of ports, so per-service ports could not work behind TLS. The UI has no server logic, so it can run anywhere. The side effect, found only on 28 October with OGC's test suite: services built links without their /api/… prefix, until each was told its public prefix ([#plugfest-and-interoperability])
              22 Oct The repository was narrowed to this testbed only, removing earlier mock-only flows Flows such as vendor binding, semantic alignment and partner pricing only ever existed as mock-ups. Removing them makes clear what the testbed actually demonstrates
              22 Oct Data ingredients are declared with standard CWL File + format and passed by reference: the process fetches the data itself Before, the UI knew a recipe's data inputs from a hard-coded list per recipe, so a recipe saved in a playground slot could never have one; the roofer recipe looked as if it ran without data. We rejected a new extension field (CWL already has File) and reading it from process descriptions (roofer states it only in prose, finding 11)
              22 Oct Process-specific input shapes are handled in the recipe, with standard CWL valueFrom; the Cook recognises a fixed set of patterns and never runs JavaScript Even with File, roofer wanted [{"kind": "url", "url": …}] rather than an OGC href. CWL expressions are JavaScript; a small cook either embeds a JavaScript engine or, like ours, supports a subset (finding 5)
              22 Oct nldt:jsonSchema: an optional bridge for parameters CWL's types cannot describe CWL cannot express oneOf, const or pattern, which roofer's inputs use. Its weakness, a pasted copy that goes stale, led to the 28 October change below
              22 Oct Extension fields renamed from x-… to a declared nldt: namespace; the old names are rejected outright cwltool --validate rejected every recipe: CWL only allows extension fields with a declared prefix. Silently ignoring the old names would have dropped a step's process link and failed only at run time. Lesson: deploying a format change means re-storing every recipe (finding 6)
              27 Oct A failed run says whose failure it is: the Cook's, the Registry's or a step's app A recipe calls processes the hub does not control; users could not tell a platform fault from a third-party one ([#where-a-failure-came-from])
              27 Oct Job addresses returned by a remote process are resolved relative to that process's own address Some servers answer with a relative /jobs/{jobID}, which breaks polling. Chaining processes from different vendors surfaces small differences like this
              28 Oct One naming rule: an app is named by its CWL Operation label, a step by its step label Each screen showed whatever name was nearest: the step's key, or a title from the remote process description; the recipe's own app name never reached the UI. Once apps can be swapped, the step and its app must have separate names ([#standard-cwl-we-use], finding 10)
              28 Oct The Wizard completes the parameter form from each app's own OGC API Process description, at display time; the recipe always wins Copying schemas into recipes goes stale. Process descriptions already carry titles, descriptions, defaults and limits in standard fields ([#parameters-cwl-types-vs-json-schema], finding 17)
              28 Oct Our OGC API Processes services tested with OGC's official test suite, and fixed: OGC's error format, links to the API definition, complete process and job lists, result-not-ready Until then we had only checked them by hand and with our own clients, which were lenient. The suite found gaps every service shared; it also showed that most of its execute tests need OGC's echo test process, which a service with specific inputs cannot replace ([#plugfest-and-interoperability])
              28 Oct (realisation) internalHref is a deployment detail that leaked into the recipe format; we recommend dropping it (not yet done) It works, but a recipe should name which process to call, not how one installation reaches it ([#extensions-we-needed])
              28 Oct (realisation) Authentication, delegated access and pricing are the main gaps between this sandbox and real use Out of scope by design, but every step of the build assumed open, free apps. A real cook would need delegated access to each app, and cooks are only interchangeable once that can be delegated (findings 23–25)

              C.16.3 Examples

              In the examples, $B stands for https://nldt-tb2.clearly.app/api.

              Call app 1 (add) directly:

              curl -X POST "$B/tools/add/processes/add-two-numbers/execution" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"inputs": {"a": 2, "b": 3}}'
# → {"outputs": {"sum": 5.0}}

              Call app 2 (multiply) directly:

              curl -X POST "$B/tools/multiply/processes/multiply-two-numbers/execution" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"inputs": {"x": 5, "y": 4}}'
# → {"outputs": {"product": 20.0}}

              Buffer a geometry, given directly (a point in Rotterdam, by 100 m):

              curl -X POST "$B/tools/geo-buffer/processes/buffer-geometry/execution" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"inputs": {"geometry": {"type": "Point", "coordinates": [4.4777, 51.9244]}, "distance": 100}}'
# → {"outputs": {"bufferedGeometry": {"type": "Feature", "geometry": {"type": "Polygon", …}}}}

              Buffer a geometry, given by reference (Rotterdam's outline, fetched live from PDOK through Hub Datasets, by 500 m). The app downloads the data itself:

              curl -X POST "$B/tools/geo-buffer/processes/buffer-geometry/execution" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"inputs": {
        "geometry": {"href": "'"$B"'/hub/datasets/ds-pdok-rotterdam-boundary/resources/res-pdok-rotterdam-outline/data",
                     "type": "application/geo+json"},
        "distance": 500}}'

              Run a recipe and wait for the result (recipe-add-multiply-demo, (2 + 3) × 4):

              curl -X POST "$B/orchestrator/processes/cook/execution" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"inputs": {"recipeId": "recipe-add-multiply-demo", "parameters": {"a": 2, "b": 3, "c": 4}}}'
# → {"result": {"result": 20.0},
# "trace": [{"step": "add", "process_id": "add-two-numbers", "inputs": {"a": 2, "b": 3}, "outputs": {"sum": 5.0}, …},
# {"step": "multiply", "process_id": "multiply-two-numbers", "inputs": {"x": 5.0, "y": 4}, "outputs": {"product": 20.0}, …}]}

              The trace shows the piping: add's sum (5) became multiply's x.

              Run the same recipe with an app swapped (multiply → multiply three numbers, which needs a third value, z). The recipe itself is not changed:

              curl -X POST "$B/orchestrator/processes/cook/execution" \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"inputs": {"recipeId": "recipe-add-multiply-demo",
                  "parameters": {"a": 2, "b": 3, "c": 4, "z": 10},
                  "toolOverrides": {"multiply": "multiply-three-numbers"}}}'
# → {"result": {"result": 200.0}, …}

              To see which parameters a swap adds before running it, ask for the effective plan (endpoint 7) with the same toolOverrides.

              Run a recipe as a job, with a data input (recipe-rotterdam-buffer-demo, buffering Rotterdam's outline by 250 m). A data input is a CWL File: a link to the data, never the data itself. This is how the App Store UI runs every recipe:

        "geometry": {"class": "File",
                     "location": "'"$B"'/hub/datasets/ds-pdok-rotterdam-boundary/resources/res-pdok-rotterdam-outline/data",
                     "format": "https://www.iana.org/assignments/media-types/application/geo+json"},
        "bufferDistance": 250}}}'
# → HTTP/1.1 201 Created
# Location: https://nldt-tb2.clearly.app/api/orchestrator/jobs/85e1ea92-…
# {"jobID": "85e1ea92-…", "processID": "cook", "status": "accepted", "recipeId": "recipe-rotterdam-buffer-demo", …}
curl "$B/orchestrator/jobs/85e1ea92-…"            # repeat every few seconds
# → {"status": "running", …}  then
# {"status": "successful", "progress": 100, "message": "1 of 1 steps done",
# "steps": [{"id": "buffer", "label": "Step 1 — Buffer", "processId": "buffer-geometry", "status": "successful", …}], …}
curl "$B/orchestrator/jobs/85e1ea92-…/results"
# → {"result": {"bufferedOutline": {"type": "Feature", "geometry": {"type": "Polygon", …}}}, "trace": […]}

              List all jobs of the Cook:

              curl "$B/orchestrator/jobs"
# → {"jobs": [{"jobID": "…", "status": "successful", "recipeId": "playground-1", …}, …]}

              D. Topic 4: Varidian

              D.1 Introduction

              Varidian started in May 2026 with creating a registry. The initial goal of this registry was to provide a standardized interface for connecting with multiple data sources. All communication would go through the registry and data source-specific logic only has to exist inside this registry and its connected microservices instead of inside every individual client. Quickly after the initial working version, the need arose to also be able to execute processes using a standardized interface. Both registry implementations for these features were based on the OGC Features and OGC Processes API’s; and after finalizing the initial implementation for the processes interface in the registry the need for chaining processes became abundantly clear. This is when the overlap between the registry’s roadmap and Research topic #4: App-Store / Recipes of the testbed was observed. We are building a catalog of collections and processes to fill the gaps in information and analysis that our own solutions run into. This testbed lets us find out whether recipes can be added to this catalog, where recipes are a combination of data-sources (ingredients) and operations in the form of an ordered list of processes (like cooking instructions. An important aspect of experiencing whether recipes fit in our catalog is to determine whether stating requirements about a recipe up front makes it possible to say something about the quality of the results that come out. Without reliable outcome, the selection of the most applicable or trustworthy recipe comes down to luck or expertise. Since we want to be able to use the results as information source for our own solutions, needing either luck or expertise means it is not ready to be integrated. By researching which metadata is required to enrich both the source data and the process’s inputs and outputs we hope to find a way to make a recipe’s results readable and trustworthy, which is exactly the focus of this research topic.

              D.1.1 Research question

              To understand our research question, some background knowledge is required. Data sources, processes and recipes all describe themselves using metadata facets. There are roughly two different types of facets:

              1. Properties which the provider sets, like the provider’s name, how old the data is, when the data is updated, etc.
              2. Properties which can’t be set by the provider, but come from the system which hosts the data, for example an App-Store. Think of how many times a data source is used or an average rating based on a review system.

              The OGC standard relies on the DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 standard for the definition of the metadata. Like the OGC standard it allows a great deal of flexibility, only a handful of properties are required to comply. At the start of the project, the focus of our research was on how the following questions could be answered:

              • Which metadata facets does an App-Store need beyond DCAT-AP-NL 3.0?
              • Which of these facets can a publisher simply write down, and which can only be observed once the system runs?
              • Can the quality of a composed answer be judged before, and/or after it is generated?

              These three questions are all based on missing facets and this was a big part of the research. The remaining two are focused on the recipe:

              • Can a recipe be generated or does it need to be created by an expert?
              • Where does Arazzo fit into the OGC API Processes idiom, and where doesn’t it fit in?

              The idea was that you could specify additional properties which would make it possible for a user to select and connect processes and data sources to configure a recipe. However, we quickly discovered that since a recipe is a black box and only the end-result is sent to the user, the actual question we want to answer is:

              • What information is needed for an end user to validate and ultimately trust the result they receive from a recipe?

              D.1.2 Use case

              To find an answer to the research question, it was important for us to work on a real-world scenario where a recipe could be implemented. We started out with the idea of creating a recipe which could answer the question “What does a cloudburst do to a city's accessibility?”. A question that can only be answered by combining data and calculations that belong to different parties. In this case we would be using two models, a hydrological model which computes where water collects and how deep it stands, and a mobility model which computes what happens to traffic once the affected road segments become impassable. A recipe would combine both models to answer the question.

              Unfortunately, we were unable to use the two models for a variety of reasons, so we shifted to another use case which is a very pressing issue in the Netherlands. That is, the question “Can I construct buildings here?”, focusing on the issue of subsidence. For the recipe to answer this question, three processes were required; one to gather the groundwater level(s) of the provided area, a second to calculate the subsidence of this area, and a third to analyze the subsidence result and validate its values against a provided threshold value.

              The subsidence process was mainly developed by IMAGEM, which uses the Deltares Atlantis API. We tuned the inputs so we could use it for our research topic. The other two processes we build ourselves; for the groundwater level we used the dataset from BRO, while we build the GeoTIFF analyzer from scratch.

              D.2 Approach

              To formulate an answer to the research question, we started from our existing platform. To evaluate the new concept of recipes, we integrated the recipes by wrapping them in processes within a newly created Cook service. To avoid having to create a custom recipe language or a workflow runner, the initial focus was on finding a recipe language that would be mature and expressive enough to define a useful recipe flow. Using the integrated recipes allowed us to test and experience what parts do and do not work when defining and using a recipe, and how its metadata can be used to determine both the recipe’s usefulness for a specific use case and the credibility of its outputs. The resulting insights gave us the set of data points required to do so, allowing us to suggest how the DCAT-AP-NL 3.0 can be improved upon.

              D.2.1 The platform

              We chose our platform as a starting point, since it already consisted of a registry component to which microservices can make themselves known and available. On top of the registered services this registry component behaves as a proxy when fetching feature collections and executing processes, it also serves all collections and processes’ metadata and adds an auth layer validating a user’s roles whenever required. A single service can handle multiple collections as well as processes. After self-registration of a service, all its collections and processes’ metadata are registered inside the registry’s catalog. For each collection and process there is granular control over which user roles can view the resource and which user roles can access its contents. This also means the collections and processes visible to a caller are generated upon request.

              Our motivation for expanding the platform with recipes is trifold.

              First, reusability. Our aim is to have collections and processes in terms of reusable building blocks for data sources and functionalities; thus, ensuring that work done for project A can also be used for project B. Each building block is usable inside a recipe, no matter whether the recipe for project A answers a different question than the recipe for project B. Because the communication layer and its definitions always adhere to the recipe’s (and OGC’s) standards, these building blocks are interchangeable.

              Second, abstraction. Allow business engineers and domain experts to develop and configure solutions based on customer needs without the help of a developer. This could support defining customer-specific functionalities by deploying a process which is defined by a recipe, without having to create a new build of the registry or a service.

              And third, runtime flexibility. Support use cases where, for instance, user A wants to perform sequential steps where data source X is used, and user B wants to perform the same sequential steps but with data source Y. Being able to define a recipe where one or more data sources can be determined during runtime and/or during an installation phase means that the recipe only has to be created once; but would still allow for enough flexibility to support both individual user’s and multiple customer’s needs.

              D.2.2 Recipe definition language

              To define a recipe and its steps, it mattered to determine upfront whether there are already existing workflow definition languages available that would be feature-rich enough for the initial research phase. This would ensure that there was no need to develop such a language ourselves, and that we could focus on answering the research question.

              D.2.2.1 Language selection

              Initially, we started out with the suggested Arazzo as the language for creating a recipe. However, the more we worked towards implementing the envisioned recipe, the more we struggled in creating a working version. This led to us creating a list of requirements the language should support based on the recipe implementation flow we were trying to create. Allowing us to determine which language and its runner(s) were the best match based on the language with its highest score. We scored four different languages: Arazzo, CWL, OpenEO and WDL. This shortlist was created based on the proposed standards from the Testbed’s engineering report document, as well as on input from other testbed participants during the first plugfest, and our own research into workflow definition languages.

              D.2.2.2 Requirements

              To find the language most fit for our research, we applied the following criteria:

              Control flow: Being able to define a control flow was a must-have. This meant the language needs to support if-statements and iterations such as a for-loop or a scatter-gather pattern.

              Parameter linking: The recipe we had envisioned executes multiple OGC processes sequentially, chaining the output of one request to the inputs of the following request. This meant that we wanted to be able to reference one process and its outputs when executing a second process.

              Multiple output parameters: Since an OGC process can have multiple output parameters, the language has to support defining and using all these outputs; so that they can be referenced individually at a later stage in the recipe.

              Reading a recipe’s output: After successfully executing a recipe with a runner, the recipe’s result must be able to be read by whatever process triggered the recipe run. This ensures the recipe’s result can be used outside a recipe runner, for example to send the recipe’s results as API response or save them as an executed OGC process its job’s results. For this to be as transparent as possible, it was desired that we can also define the recipe’s result outputs.

              Reusable components: Because there are functionalities which follow a shared pattern or are very similar in their core, it is desirable to have a recipe language with support for reusable components or modules. This helps in defining a flow like executing an OGC process and fetching its results whenever it’s finished successfully in a uniform way. And it eliminates the need to keep reimplementing the same three blocks for job execution, polling for success and results fetching.

              Intermediate logging & updates: Recipes consist of multiple steps performed sequentially or in parallel. To be able to follow the workflow’s progress and understand each individual step, support is needed for keeping track of logs and updating the status of each of these steps. This makes developing and debugging a recipe easier but also allows for visualizing a recipe and its progress.

              Machine Readable: Ideally, a recipe definition should be machine-readable. This means a computer can analyze a recipe and determine its relevance to use cases, as well as read, execute, alter, or work with a recipe in other ways. Another important use case for machine readability is the use inside AI agents and/or automations.

              Error Handling: Chaining processes means we need to be aware of failures and where they happened. This also means the recipe is not only a happy flow, describing which processes and data sources are chained if everything goes well, but it also needs to describe what is passed on to the end-user whenever a failure occurs.

              D.2.2.3 Best suited: WDL and Sprocket runner

              After considering four different workflow languages and their runners, it was ultimately decided that WDL with the Sprocket runner was best suited to implement the recipe we had envisioned. The three other languages had their shortcomings which could not be worked around, whereas WDL was most flexible in what we were trying to achieve. See our considerations in the scoring sheet from Appendix A.

              D.2.3 Created recipes

              Figure 1: The three created recipes

              After picking WDL as the recipe definition language for our research, we created three WDL files that each represented a recipe. These recipes all have the same output definition but differ in their input definitions and underlying OGC processes usage. Instead of having to analyze the subsidence result output for oneself, the recipes will return only the areas where the subsidence results are below a threshold value. This means the question “Can I construct buildings here?” is answered by each recipe with a simple “Only in these areas” when executed.

              D.2.3.1 Subsidence Analysis Recipe

              The Subsidence Analysis Recipe is the minimal implementation of a recipe for the determined scope. The user needs to supply all inputs by hand. Inputs defined are the groundwater level, the surcharge thickness, the subsidence result threshold, and the areas on which the analysis is performed. The groundwater level input is used as-is for the subsidence calculation process, and this calculation result is passed on to the Geotiff Analyzer process to validate each subsidence value based on the threshold input value.

              D.2.3.2 Expert Site Analysis Recipe

              With the Expert Site Analysis recipe, the groundwater level input is replaced with an OGC process that retrieves groundwater monitoring measurements registered in the BRO Key Registry of the Subsurface, within the provided surcharge analysis areas. While the groundwater level input is retrieved from an external system, the user still must provide inputs by hand. They now have to provide up to five additional inputs required by the BRO groundwater monitoring registry: statistic, datetime, maxScreenDepth, observationTypes, and statuses.

              D.2.3.3 Soil-Guided Site Analysis Recipe

              The underlying idea of the Soil-Guided Site Analysis Recipe is that an expert would check the input parameters of the Expert Site Analysis Recipe and predefine as many of the recipe’s inputs so that the end user does not have to provide them or have knowledge about what they mean or do. With the created recipe, this means that the user only needs to provide the areas for the surcharge analysis. Other recipe inputs such as statistic, datetime, maxScreenDepth, observationTypes, statuses, thickness, and threshold are all hidden from the user, as they have predefined values within the recipe. This results in a very simple recipe execution call.

              D.2.4 Platform expanded with recipes

              Figure 2: Our platform including the newly added cook service

              The registry inside our platform did not need any alterations to support running recipes, apart from general improvements to the OGC API standards conformance. To support recipes written in WDL, a cook service was added which holds all logic and knowledge about these recipes; it defines where their WDL definitions are and how the Sprocket runner can be called to run a recipe. The recipe’s process interface is the same as any other process. Only underneath the surface there is additional logic defined about what process calls are chained and how their outputs and inputs are passed on to each other. All of this is defined using WDL, and the Sprocket runner reads, validates and executes this WDL file and provides the execution’s output(s) as a result back to the process inside the cook service.

              Defining recipes in WDL allowed us to perform sequential process executions without individual process calls. From a user standpoint, there is only one process answering their query. The specific data sources and intermediate processes are hidden from the user, as these details are irrelevant to their workflow. Instead, the user simply provides the inputs required by the WDL definition, which the cook service then passes to the Sprocket runner.

              D.3 Results

              Our full facet analysis can be found in Appendix C, here we will try to recap our findings.

              D.3.1 Facet analysis: what does an end user need to trust a workflow result?

              We chain three processes into three recipes that all answer the same question: can I build here? The recipes return the same output but differ in who decides the inputs: the user, a process, or the recipe author.

              We started from the processes. For each one we asked what we would need to know to trust its result and added the questions that came up while running the recipes. We then compared the recipes to see which questions appear or disappear as inputs move away from the user. Only after that did we group the questions into facets and check them against DCAT-AP-NL and related standards.

              Grouping the questions showed that the clearest gaps are not in the catalogue but in the description of a component: what its inputs and outputs mean, and which question it can answer. We had discussed using NEN 2660 and libraries built on it, such as IMBOR and the RWS OTL, for this. To test that idea, we described our own case, realizing a dwelling, in that style. The example showed something the questions alone did not: our recipes check one requirement of the activity, not whether you can build there in general.

              The sheets follow that order: What processes tell us, What recipes change, what should be where, What NEN 2660 adds, What we recommend.

              D.3.1.1 Recommendations

              1. Collect the metadata of every step for each job. When a recipe or process determines a value for the user, the user wants to know where that value came from. The process fetching groundwater level(s) returns the wells and criteria it used, but the recipe drops them. The subsidence process says nothing about the model, version or subsurface data it used. Every job should therefore produce a job record that holds, for each step, what was used and what happened. A recipe’s job record contains the job records of the processes it called. Owners may restrict their metadata, but the job record still shows that the step exists and who to ask. The recipe author remains accountable for the recipe.

              2. Keep fixed inputs visible. The Soil Guided Site Analysis recipe defines seven inputs in code, so the user cannot see which choices were made for them. Its threshold of 0.4 also differs from the process default of 0.25 and has no source declaration. A fixed input should stay in the recipe’s description with its value, marked as not editable, together with who fixed it, why, and where the value comes from.

              3. Describe the meaning and unit of every input and output. The groundwater level input is only described as ‘m NAP’ in free text, and the subsidence process’ outputs and the threshold have no unit at all. We linked the steps by hand, with nothing to check that they mean the same thing. Every input and output should refer to a concept and a unit, so a platform can check that one step’s output fits the next step’s input.

              4. Describe how a process behaves at its limits. The groundwater-level process stops when no well meets the criteria in the provided area. The geotiff-analyzer process counts a value exactly at the threshold as ‘above’. The subsidence process advertises a default it does not use. None of this is described anywhere. A process description should state:

                • its validity domain;
                • what counts as missing data, and what the process then does;
                • how it treats a value at a threshold;
                • which defaults apply.

                Failures should use shared error concepts, so a recipe can pass on a message the user understands.

              5. Raise obligation levels for components used in workflows. Most properties that answer our questions already exist in DCAT-AP-NL but are optional; for example, dct:modified, dct:source, dcat:spatialResolutionInMeters and dcat:version. A record can be valid and still answer none of our questions.

              6. Add a conformance profile on top of DCAT-AP-NL. When a property is missing, a user cannot tell whether the value is unknown or simply not given, and ‘no period given’ is not the same as ‘all periods’. A record that declares the profile guarantees that the required properties are present, so a missing value means the record does not conform. The platform can check the profile before a recipe runs and show what is missing per step.

              7. Let each component declare which question it can answer. We connected the steps because we knew what each one delivers, but nothing in the descriptions says so. Each component should declare which properties of which object types it needs and delivers, in terms of a shared library based on NEN 2660, such as IMBOR or the RWS OTL. A recipe should also declare the activity and the requirement it checks. A user then knows what kind of answer to expect, and what is not checked.

              Recommendations 3, 4 and 7 are for process providers, and 2 and 7 for recipe authors. Recommendation 1 is for the platform. Recommendations 5 and 6 are for the maintainers of DCAT-AP-NL.

              D.4 Discussion

              We would like to discuss two different aspects; How we see the lifecycle of a recipe and the step from a recipe to triggered workflows.

              D.4.1 How do we see the lifecycle of a recipe

              A recipe will have a lifecycle; someone uses data sources and processes to find an answer to a question and decides to turn this into a flow other people can use. Once more people start using the flow, you probably discover it doesn’t work all the time, or data sources and processes don’t function as you expected, so you set out to improve the flow. You find better sources, either better accessible or with better quality data. You need to add error handling. What needs to happen when one data source fails or returns limited data, do you fail the flow, or can we continue?

              D.4.1.1 Writing a recipe

              In our view you need to be an expert in the field of the recipe to be able to define it, knowing which data is valid and how to determine that is crucial. Once an expert has created a recipe, or maybe a developer does that using some specs, the role of the expert is not finished. They will need to validate the recipe works and give it their stamp of approval. Trusting if a recipe returns valid data depends a great deal on who has generated or at least reviewed the script and the results. It’s the reason why apps like Uber became popular; each part of the cycle is validated or approved by each party. For a recipe we can see multiple players:

              • The used data sources; if they would get feedback about who is using their data and how, they could add significant insights to whether the data is valid for that application.
              • The processes in the different steps; they could monitor the data they receive as input and be verbose about the expected quality of the results. If they can see how their processes are used, they too can provide significant insights about whether the output can be used for the given use-case or not.
              • The experts who wire the recipe; they should be able to add requirements for the data quality, being able to check the results and implement this for the end user.

              In our view, writing a recipe is not a task to be taken lightly. If a recipe is just a workflow where you quickly stick some data sources and processes together, your only hope would be that the sources for the data and process are taking their job very serious and can somehow know exactly what kind of information you need to be able to connect them together.

              D.4.1.2 Installation

              Once you have a recipe that works, has error handling and feels finished, the recipe is deployed. For the testbed we manually wrapped the recipe inside a process, which we believe could be automated in the future. We do this because the results of a process and the results of a recipe have very similar outputs and by wrapping it as a process, we could run the recipe inside the IMAGEM client and show the results.

              D.4.1.3 Usage

              Once installed anybody who can access the recipe can call it. Very quickly errors which seem to make perfect sense to the expert and the writer of the recipe lack the required clarity. For example; when you draw a region just outside the data provided by GeoTop, which is used by the Subsidence Analyses, the error reads Atlantis subsidence calculation failed with status 400 Bad Request: {"error":"Invalid input"}. This is the error of the process and clear at the moment of building the recipe, but we would much rather see an error: Atlantis subsidence calculation failed because the target area is outside our data-source. To make this happen the recipe would need far better error-handling capability than we have currently created.

              D.4.2 From recipes to triggered workflows

              Working with WDL showed us that control flow is not the hard part of a recipe. Branching, iteration and error handling were all possible. What was missing was everything around the flow: why a step runs, under which conditions, what it can produce, and what happened during a run. A recipe executed by a script runner is a long-running black box. When a single polling step failed in our Soil Guided Site Analysis recipe, the whole run stopped with an error only a developer could read, and nothing in between was visible to the user.

              For that reason, we are building our own execution model on top of our registry. It is based on workflows rather than recipes, and on triggers as the underlying mechanism.

              D.4.2.1 Workflows as the design, triggers as the mechanism

              A workflow is designed as a graph of elements connected by arrows, comparable to a BPMN process. The graph is the source of truth: it is what the author draws, reviews and approves.

              A workflow is not a single process, every arrow that becomes active results in a trigger stored in the registry. A runner picks up the trigger, executes one element, stores the result, and places new triggers for the outgoing arrows. A workflow is therefore never a long-running process. It is a sequence of short, independent executions that each leave a record behind. Waiting costs nothing: a manual expert review that takes three days is simply a trigger that has not fired yet.

              D.4.2.2 Context conditions

              A plain workflow describes a fixed process. Many real questions need more flexibility: some steps are only relevant in certain situations, some are optional, and some must be repeated for each item. Case management standards such as CMMN describe this as dynamic case management. We add it to our workflows with a single concept: a condition on a trigger.

              When a conditional trigger fires, the first thing it does is evaluate the condition against the current state of the case. If the condition holds, the trigger places a new trigger without the condition, and the step is executed. If it does not hold, no new trigger is placed. In both cases the evaluation, and the values it was based on, are stored.

              Conditions can refer to the context of the question, such as the object, its function and the planned work activity. They can also refer to facets of the data and processes used, such as resolution or age.

              D.4.2.3 Outcomes

              Every element declares which outcomes it can produce, and each outcome is a separate exit in the user interface. A condition has three outcomes: true, false and unknown. The last one occurs when the information needed to evaluate the condition is missing, for example when a data source does not declare its resolution. A process declares its own domain outcomes. The groundwater process, for instance, can return found, no monitoring well in area or no approved measurements.

              Technical errors, such as timeouts or an unavailable service, are retried by the runner. Only a definitive failure becomes an outcome.

              This changes error handling from code into design. The subsidence error we encountered (“400 Bad Request: Invalid input” when drawing outside the GeoTOP coverage) becomes an outcome area outside coverage error. The author can connect it to a clear message for the user, an alternative source or an expert review. Because outcomes are declared, the editor can also check whether a workflow is complete: every outcome is either connected or explicitly marked as an end point.

              OGC API Processes do not support this, they only report job status (successful, failed, dismissed) with free-text details. Processes cannot declare their possible outcomes in a machine-readable way, which we consider a gap.

              D.4.2.4 Joining paths

              When several paths come together, the next element runs as soon as no triggers upstream of it are still waiting or running. This removes the need to track which paths were skipped: a path whose condition was false simply produces no further triggers. Two conditions apply. Completing an element and placing its next triggers must happen as one atomic step. Triggers that wait for a condition to become true need an expiry time, so a join can never wait forever.

              D.4.2.5 Records as the basis for trust

              Every execution stores what it did: the trigger that started it, the input it used, the condition values it evaluated, and the outcome it produced. The chain of triggers forms the lineage of the result. It shows which sources were used, which steps were executed or skipped, and why.

              This is how the execution model answers our research question. In the facet analysis we asked what information an end user needs to trust the result of a black box. Part of the answer lies in the metadata of data and processes: their context, quality and declared outcomes. The other part lies in the execution itself. A result can only be trusted if every step that produced it can be followed and explained, including the steps that did not run.

              D.5 Conclusions

              We have learned a lot about recipes, workflows as well as the OGC Processes API during the testbed. We still believe recipes have a big place in our registry, but we also see the danger that unless the recipes are fully transparent the results won’t qualify as trustworthy information. Just like processes, they need to be created like a piece of software, with error handling, tests and clear expectations documenting use- and edge-cases.

              The idea that a recipe could be selected and different data sources and processes can be wired up needs a lot of consideration. Without that the receiver of the data will select and run the recipe with data resulting in wrong insights. Adding the suggested facets will help, but the recipe still needs a way to require that data.

              D.5.1 Plugfests

              We consider both plugfests useful additions to the testbed. In the first plugfest the discussions gave us a place to state our shortcomings with Arazzo to see if others had similar experiences, as well as a new direction to investigate workflow languages besides it such as OpenEO and CWL. During the second plugfest we split into two teams, giving us a seat in two rooms. I think this is something more teams should do, since it felt to us that different subjects were discussed in the different rooms. The final insights of the second plugfest did lead to some additional fixes to our processes.

              The timing of the plugfest also helped us, the first one helped us finish preliminary research, while the second allowed us to check if there was anything we had missed.

              D.5.2 OGC standards

              Because we see a lot of our day-to-day work heavily related to geo-data, we will continue working with the OGC API standards. The interaction with Geonovum was very positive, and we will keep them informed about our future developments as well as ask them to explain the workings of other OGC standards, like the Records and Tiles.

              E.5.1 interval is a nested array (sources B, A; spec, planned)

              time.interval and layers[].temporal.interval are defined as "null, or an array containing exactly one array of exactly two date strings". The shape comes from OGC API Features/Records extent.temporal. In practice it is awkward in typed environments, it looks like a list while the spec allows only one entry, and our own writer already produces values that break it.

              The schema currently reads (Tools/ScxCesiumViewer/schema/scx.schema.json, $defs/interval):

              "interval": {
  "description": "OGC API extent.temporal: [[begin, einde]], null of \"..\" = open",
  "type": "array",
  "minItems": 1,
  "items": {
    "type": "array",
    "minItems": 2,
    "maxItems": 2,
    "items": { "type": ["string", "null"] }
  }
}

              A valid value then looks like this:

              "temporal": { "interval": [["2025-10-01T08:00:00Z", "2025-11-01T08:00:00Z"]], "trs": "http://www.opengis.net/def/uom/ISO-8601/0/Gregorian" }

              with ".." for an open end:

              "temporal": { "interval": [["2025-10-01T08:00:00Z", ".."]] }

              Blueprints cannot hold TArray<TArray<FString>>. For the importer we needed a wrapper UObject that only holds the inner array (Script/ManagedDigitalTwin/Geonovum/UCS_NestedStringArray.cs):

              [UClass]
public partial class UCS_NestedStringArray : UObject
{
    [UProperty(PropertyFlags.BlueprintReadWrite)]
    public partial IList<string> Items { get; set; }
}

              The document model therefore has IList<UCS_NestedStringArray> instead of a start/end pair (UCS_ScxDocLayerTemporal.cs):

              [UProperty(PropertyFlags.BlueprintReadWrite)]
[JsonPropertyName("interval")] public partial IList<UCS_NestedStringArray> Interval { get; set; }

              Reading the start date in a Blueprint is Interval → Get (index 0) → Items → Get (index 0) on an object reference. With an object form it would be a single Start pin. We expect the same friction in other typed languages (C#, C++, Java, Kotlin, Swift): it is a pair of two values written as a list of lists.

              The reader has to be written by hand. System.Text.Json cannot deserialise into UnrealSharp UObject lists, so the time converter walks the tokens itself, with an extra inner loop just for the second array level (Json/UCS_ScxDocTimeConverter.cs, Read):

              case "interval":
    interval = [];
    while (reader.Read())
    {
        if (reader.TokenType == JsonTokenType.EndArray) { break; }

              E.5.2 Camera: field of view, clipping planes and projection (sources C, B; spec, fov implemented)

              Position, heading and tilt transfer correctly, but the resulting image differs per viewer because the lens is not described. We added fov, which fixes the zoom. Near/far clipping and orthographic versus perspective projection are still missing.

              Same camera, three lenses (measured 9 Oct, Merwehaven):

              Viewer Default horizontal FOV Visible ground width at 310 m range

...

              In CesiumJS (Tools/ScxCesiumViewer/index.html) frustum.fov applies to the wider canvas dimension, so a horizontal value has to be converted for a portrait canvas:

              C.viewer.camera.frustum.fov = aspect >= 1
  ? Cesium.Math.toRadians(fovH)
  : 2 * Math.atan(Math.tan(Cesium.Math.toRadians(fovH) / 2) / aspect);

              In ArcGIS camera.fov is diagonal, so we convert horizontal to diagonal using the pane aspect ratio:

              const diag = 2 * Math.atan(Math.tan(fovH * Math.PI / 360) * Math.sqrt(1 + (h / w) * (h / w)));

              Resulting fragment in the 16 Oct export:

              "view": { "heading": 60.368, "tilt": 61.565, "fov": 90, "position": { ... } }

              The three engines use three definitions of fov (horizontal, wider axis, diagonal). The spec has to say which one SCX means. We chose horizontal, in degrees.

              Still missing (B). The same model exists in JSON in BCF-API 3.0, section 3.5. The fragment below uses the BCF-API JSON names (BCF-XML uses PerspectiveCamera, CameraViewPoint, FieldOfView etc.); the values are placeholders.               Show the BCF camera fragment (19 lines)

              E.5.6 The same 3D Tiles behave differently per viewer: invalid glTF, unsupported content and a vertical offset in ArcGIS (source C; data-side, spec)

              Revised 16 Oct. On 9 Oct the ArcGIS pane showed buildings "lower and not in the right place", and we put that down to the 3D Tiles 1.0 up-axis rule. That explanation was wrong. On 16 Oct, with ArcGIS Maps SDK 4.33.11 and both panes driven by the same camera in headless Chrome, the M4H-FI b3dm renders in ArcGIS exactly where CesiumJS renders it, and a diagnostic proxy that rewrites asset.gltfUpAxis (Y, Z, removed) makes no difference. We found three other causes:

              • (a) Invalid glTF in the building tiles. All 1309 rotterdam-gebouwen-cm tiles declare emissiveFactor with four components, where glTF 2.0 requires exactly three. CesiumJS and Cesium for Unreal accept it. The ArcGIS loader drops the primitive without an error: the layer reports loaded, fetches the tiles and draws nothing. With the array cut to three components, ArcGIS draws the buildings on the same footprints as CesiumJS.
              • (b) A vertical offset of about 41 m in ArcGIS. ArcGIS draws the ECEF tile geometry about 41 m below its draped basemap; CesiumJS draws it on the imagery. Seen from an oblique camera the buildings then appear shifted against the aerial photo. The offset is close to the geoid height at Rotterdam ($\approx 42\text{ m}$). With elevationInfo.offset = +41 in ArcGIS the two panes coincide.
              • (c) ArcGIS cannot render the instanced tree tiles. rotterdam-bomen-cm consists of cmpt composites of i3dm instances. ArcGIS supports neither. After conversion to 3D Tiles 1.1 glb with EXT_mesh_gpu_instancing it still draws nothing, and a tileset that uses 1.1 contents arrays is refused. CesiumJS renders both the original and the converted set.

              The document carries no content type, version or vertical datum, so a viewer cannot warn about any of this. It just shows a different picture.

              emissiveFactor has four components; glTF 2.0 requires three. The M4H-FI tile (generator 4.6.5, 2015–2025) has [0, 0, 0] and renders.               (b) The vertical offset. Near-nadir test (tilt 3°, 650 m, camera over 51.9049 N 4.4231 E) with the repaired buildings in ArcGIS and the original in CesiumJS, and the same PDOK orthophoto in both panes. The tileset-centre marker lands on the same photo feature (a pier tip) in both panes, so the imagery is registered identically. In CesiumJS the building footprints match the roofs. In ArcGIS they are shifted radially away from the image centre, up to about 20 m at 300 m from nadir. Seen from above, a radial shift like that points to a height error: $\Delta x = \Delta h \cdot d / H$ gives $\Delta h \approx 20 \cdot 650 / 300 \approx 43\text{ m}$. We settled the sign with two more nadir captures and a check on a full-resolution screen: layer.elevationInfo = { mode: "absolute-height", offset: +41 } puts the footprints exactly on the roofs, −41 does not. So in ArcGIS the geometry sits about 41 m below its draped ground, while in CesiumJS it sits on it.

              Our explanation, combined with the NAP heights below: the tiles carry NAP heights as if they were ellipsoidal (0–15 m). CesiumJS drapes its imagery on the ellipsoid ($h = 0$), so NAP-as-ellipsoidal data lands on the imagery. ArcGIS drapes its basemap on a flat ground at orthometric height 0, which is the geoid, about 42 m above the ellipsoid at Rotterdam (EGM96 and NLGEO2018 differ by about a metre there). The same data then ends up 41 m below the basemap. How exactly ArcGIS handles the geoid internally is an engine question; we did not verify it. For SCX the point is that the same ECEF geometry sits at a different height relative to each engine's draped ground, and the document has no way of saying which is intended. Our viewer now uses +41 m for ArcGIS by default for scenes in the Netherlands.

              E.5.8 Timestamps are UTC: the spec should say so and importers should parse them that way (source A; spec, planned)

              All SCX timestamps are ISO 8601 with a Z. The .NET parser in our importer discards that unless told otherwise, so a round trip through the DigitalTwin shifts every time by the machine's UTC offset. The spec does not say how offsets are to be handled.

              The parser call in the importer (UCS_ScxDocLayerTemporal.cs, the same in UCS_ScxDocTime.cs):

              DateTime.TryParse(Interval[0].Items[0], System.Globalization.CultureInfo.InvariantCulture.DateTimeFormat, out DateTime parsedDate)

              There is no DateTimeStyles argument. Reproduced with the same parser on the dev laptop (time zone W. Europe, +01:00, DST +02:00 on 1 Oct):

              Parse('2025-10-01T08:00:00Z')                 -> Kind=Local  value=2025-10-01T10:00:00.0000000+02:00
with DateTimeStyles.AdjustToUniversal         -> Kind=Utc    value=2025-10-01T08:00:00.0000000Z

              The DigitalTwin's FDateTime has no time zone; ToFDateTime() copies the clock time. The export then writes that clock time with a Z again:

              Step Value
              Document in 2025-10-01T08:00:00Z
              After TryParse (Kind=Local) 2025-10-01 10:00 local
              FDateTime in the twin 2025-10-01 10:00
              Export 2025-10-01T10:00:00Z

              One round trip adds 2 hours; DT → browser → DT twice adds 4. For a traffic scenario defined per hour, that moves the "after" dataset into the wrong slot.

              Proposal (spec). All date-time values are RFC 3339 / ISO 8601 with a mandatory UTC designator or offset. Writers SHOULD use Z; readers MUST honour the offset.

              Implementation in the TPG DigitalTwin.

              • Importer (B), planned: Parse with DateTimeStyles.AssumeUniversal | DateTimeStyles.AdjustToUniversal in the TryParse calls (MinDate, MaxDate in temporal; MinDate, MaxDate, CurrentDate in time) and convert to FDateTime as UTC.
              • Exporter (A): already writes Z from FDateTime taken as UTC ("current": "2026-10-16T13:00:00Z" in E.4 Scope and test setup). No change.

              E.5.9 The vertical datum rule in §4.5 holds and should stay normative (source C; spec, confirms)

              With terrain in CesiumJS and no terrain in ArcGIS, the same ellipsoidal camera altitude (CRS84h, "altitude": 333.28) ends up at visibly different heights above the ground. The decoded tiles (E.5.6 The same 3D Tiles behave differently per viewer: invalid glTF, unsupported content and a vertical offset in ArcGIS (source C; data-side, spec)) show NAP heights in data that is supposed to be ellipsoidal.

              crs in the document is http://www.opengis.net/def/crs/OGC/0/CRS84h (ellipsoidal height).               F.2 Executive summary

              Crowd simulation helps event organizers evaluate pedestrian movement and identify potential safety risks before an event takes place. Temporary event infrastructure, such as stages, barriers, entrances, and exits, is typically designed in CAD applications. Crowd simulation modelers often need to manually recreate walkable areas, obstacles, and other relevant features from these designs. This process is time-consuming, introduces opportunities for errors, and must be repeated when event designs change.

              This research investigates how SCX, a lightweight format for exchanging scene information between applications, can be extended to support crowd simulation. The extension presented in this report adds semantic information to geographic data, allowing simulation applications to distinguish between features such as walkable areas, obstacles, entrances, exits, and queues.

              The research was carried out by uCrowds and AniVizzion. AniVizzion implemented the export workflow in SCAD Re-Event, while uCrowds implemented the import workflow in SimCrowds. The demonstration showed that event designs can be transferred directly from CAD into SimCrowds, where the relevant simulation objects and a navigation mesh are created without manual redrawing.

              The workflow automates construction of the simulation environment, although users must still connect simulation objects into pedestrian flows and configure simulation parameters. Further research could improve interoperability through shared semantic definitions, investigate applications in other simulation domains, and explore closer integration between CAD design and simulation.

              F.3 1. Introduction

              Temporary infrastructure for public events, including stages, barriers, entrances, exits, and queue areas, is commonly designed in computer-aided design (CAD) applications. Crowd simulation applications use models of these event sites to evaluate pedestrian movement and support safety planning. To represent pedestrian movement, these applications need to distinguish areas where people can walk from obstacles and other inaccessible areas. Crowd models also describe destinations and intermediate goals, including points of interest such as food stalls, toilets, and viewing areas, that pedestrians may visit during an event. However, CAD designs are typically not transferred directly into simulation models. Instead, simulation modelers use CAD drawings as visual references and manually recreate walkable areas, obstacles, and other simulation-relevant features and destinations. This process takes time, introduces opportunities for interpretation errors, and must be repeated when event designs change. As a result, simulation models may also become inconsistent with the latest event design.

              Geonovum Testbed 2026, phase 2, promotes interoperability between digital twin components based on the Dutch nLDT architecture. Research Topic #5, Web 3D Context Document (export/import), investigates the exchange of scene information between independent applications. This research builds on the work of Esri Nederland in Digital Twin as a Service Testbed II, which introduced SCX, a lightweight JSON format for exchanging scene configurations, and demonstrated it between ArcGIS and Cesium. SCX describes a camera view, time settings, and references to external scene layers. The original format supports scene exchange for visualization but does not describe what a layer represents within a simulation.

              The iCAD research investigates how SCX can be extended to assign semantic meaning to georeferenced 2D scene layers, allowing simulation applications to interpret their function rather than only their geometry. In the CAD-to-crowd use case, these functions distinguish walkable areas, obstacles, entrances, exits, and queues. The objective is to enable a simulation application to construct its environment directly from an event design, without manually redrawing the relevant geometry.

              The research was carried out by a consortium of uCrowds and AniVizzion. uCrowds develops SimCrowds, a real-time crowd simulation application used to model and analyze pedestrian movement. AniVizzion develops SCAD Re-Event, a CAD application for event production and temporary infrastructure design. uCrowds led the design of the SCX extension and implemented the importer in SimCrowds, while AniVizzion implemented the exporter in SCAD Re-Event. Together, these implementations establish an exchange workflow from CAD design to crowd simulation. The demonstration also uses SimCrowds' existing Web Map Service (WMS) functionality to make simulated crowd density available to geographic visualization applications.

              The proof of concept focuses on one use case within event planning and crowd safety. It evaluates whether georeferenced 2D scene layers can be exchanged and interpreted to construct a crowd simulation environment without manual redrawing. The resulting simulation objects still require users to establish pedestrian flows and configure simulation parameters. Although the SCX extension could support other simulation domains, this research evaluates its application to crowd simulation.

              F.4 2. Background and standards

              Geonovum's Research Topic #5 builds on earlier work on exchanging 3D scene configurations between applications. This section introduces the SCX format developed in Digital Twin as a Service Testbed II, the existing CAD-to-crowd simulation workflow, and the geospatial standards relevant to the research.

              F.4.1 2.1 Scene exchange with SCX

              Geonovum describes a Web 3D Context Document as a portable, platform-independent JSON description of a scene. It contains references to geographic data sources and information needed to reconstruct a scene, such as viewpoint, time, and spatial context.

              In Digital Twin as a Service Testbed II, Esri Nederland developed SCX, a lightweight JSON schema for scene exchange. The research examined the ArcGIS Web Scene Specification and the CesiumJS scene model to identify common elements that could be exchanged between visualization applications. The resulting schema was demonstrated through scene exchange between ArcGIS and Cesium.

              The original SCX document contains three main sections:

              Section Description
              view Camera position and orientation, including heading and tilt.
              time Current scene time, with an optional time range defined by start and end.
              layers An ordered list of layers with their title, type, visibility, and source URL.

              SCX references external data sources rather than embedding geometry in the document. This keeps the scene description lightweight and independent of the underlying data formats.

              The original schema focuses on visualization. A layer's type identifies its representation or loading category, such as imagery, elevation, or scene, but does not describe its meaning within a simulation.

              F.4.2 2.2 Current CAD-to-crowd simulation workflow

              SCAD Re-Event provides detailed CAD layouts of temporary infrastructure for event planning and construction. Crowd simulation applications such as SimCrowds need the same environment described in terms of pedestrian-relevant functions. The simulation model must distinguish walkable areas from obstacles, identify locations where pedestrians enter, exit, or queue, and represent destinations or intermediate goals that pedestrians can visit.

              In the existing workflow, modelers use CAD drawings as visual references and manually recreate the relevant geometry in the simulation application. This introduces additional work and potential interpretation errors. Moreover, this workflow can lead to synchronization issues between event planning and the simulation model. When an event design changes, the simulation environment must be updated accordingly, and the two may become inconsistent until these changes are incorporated.

              Exchanging the geometry alone does not resolve this problem. The receiving application also needs the semantic meaning, i.e., the function, of the geometry to construct the corresponding simulation objects.

              F.4.3 2.3 Geographic data standards

              GeoJSON (RFC 7946) is a widely supported JSON format for geographic features. It represents points, lines, polygons, and their associated properties. GeoJSON uses WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84), with coordinates expressed in longitude, latitude order. It does not provide a standardized mechanism for declaring an alternative coordinate reference system (CRS).

              OGC Features and Geometries JSON (JSON-FG) 1.0 extends GeoJSON with additional capabilities, including support for geometry in other coordinate reference systems. Several members are relevant to this research:

              • coordRefSys identifies the coordinate reference system of the data when it differs from the default WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84).
              • place represents geometry in the identified CRS, while geometry can provide a geographic representation in the default WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84).
              • conformsTo identifies the JSON-FG conformance classes implemented by the document.

              When coordRefSys is absent, the default geographic CRS applies. OGC:CRS84 specifies longitude, latitude axis order and should not be confused with EPSG:4326, which uses latitude, longitude axis order.

              JSON-FG makes it possible to exchange georeferenced CAD-derived geometry in a projected coordinate system such as the Dutch Rijksdriehoeksstelsel (RD, EPSG:28992). Feature properties can also carry application-specific information without requiring the exchange format to standardize individual simulation parameters.

              CityJSON provides another JSON-based approach for representing semantically classified 3D city objects. However, this research focuses on exchanging georeferenced 2D functional areas rather than 3D city models, making JSON-FG a more suitable choice for the scene layer data.

              F.4.4 2.4 Context within the nLDT architecture

              Geonovum's nLDT reference architecture promotes a network of interoperable local digital twins in which data, computational models, and visualization components can operate independently and exchange information through open standards.

              The CAD-to-crowd workflow follows this approach. SCAD Re-Event provides event design data, while SimCrowds uses the exchanged information to construct an environment for crowd simulation. The simulation results can subsequently be shared with geospatial visualization applications through existing OGC services.

              SCX provides the scene-level exchange mechanism between these components. Extending it with semantic information supports the broader objective of interoperability between independent digital twin applications.

              F.5 3. Research question

              This research investigates how the SCX schema can be extended beyond scene visualization to support simulation applications. The main research question is:

              How can SCX be extended to give georeferenced scene layers semantic meaning, so that simulation applications can use them to construct simulation environments, as demonstrated through the CAD-to-crowd workflow?

              To answer this question, the research addresses three sub-questions:

              1. Semantic meaning: What information do crowd simulation applications need to interpret scene layers, and how can SCX represent this information while leaving simulation-specific details to individual applications?
              2. Georeferencing and interoperability: How can SCX exchange georeferenced 2D scene layers in different coordinate reference systems while retaining its lightweight, application-independent structure?
              3. Practical validation: To what extent does the extended SCX enable the construction of a crowd simulation environment from CAD data without manual redrawing, and what limitations remain?

              The first two sub-questions guide the design of the SCX extension. The third evaluates the extension through the implemented CAD-to-crowd workflow and identifies remaining limitations.

              F.6 4. Methodology

              The research followed an iterative, prototype-driven approach in which the SCX extension and its implementation were developed together. The work was organized around four activities: schema design, exporter development, importer development, and integration testing.

              F.6.1 4.1 Schema design

              Schema design began by identifying basic spatial functions commonly needed for pedestrian simulation: areas where people can move, obstacles that restrict movement, locations for entering and exiting, queues, and intermediate destinations. We compared these roles with the information available in SCX and the event design data from SCAD Re-Event. SimCrowds served as the implementation used to evaluate the design; interoperability with other crowd simulation tools was not tested.

              uCrowds led the design of the extension, refining it during importer development rather than finalizing it beforehand. Design decisions were informed by practical implementation challenges, a review of related standards including GeoJSON and JSON-FG, and feedback from Geonovum. Particular attention was given to separating the semantic role of scene layers from the geometry and metadata contained in external layer files.

              F.6.2 4.2 Exporter and importer development

              AniVizzion extended SCAD Re-Event with dedicated drawing elements for crowd simulation. Walkable areas, obstacles, waypoints and the terrain boundary are drawn as closed polygons on dedicated drawing layers. Entries, exits, queues and podiums are placed as parametric blocks whose width and height can be adjusted, and whose orientation defines the facing direction. During the modeling phase, designers draw these elements directly on the terrain alongside the temporary event infrastructure. Walkable areas define the terrain on which pedestrians can move, while the other elements represent functional parts of the crowd simulation environment.

              AniVizzion developed an SCX exporter that converts the modeled scene into an extended SCX document referencing georeferenced JSON-FG files, one file per function. The exporter assigns the SCX layer function from the source of the geometry: the drawing layer for polygons, or the block name for parametric blocks. Polygons nested inside other blocks are included. Function layers without any features are omitted from the export. The exported scene can optionally be uploaded from the export dialog to an AniVizzion scene server, which makes it available through a publicly accessible URL.

              uCrowds developed the corresponding importer in SimCrowds. The importer retrieves the SCX document and its referenced JSON-FG files, interprets the layer functions, and creates the corresponding simulation objects. After the import, the simulation environment and underlying datastructures are automatically generated.

              F.6.3 4.3 Validation

              Validation focused on whether the exchanged scene data could be interpreted correctly and used to construct a crowd simulation environment. The workflow was evaluated using a real-world event design for Huntenpop 2025, described in Section 6.1.

              The exported JSON-FG files were checked using Geonovum's JSON-FG OGC Checker. In SimCrowds, we tested whether the layer functions were correctly interpreted, whether the corresponding simulation objects were created, and whether a navigation mesh could be generated from the imported environment.

              We also examined which steps still required user interaction after import, particularly connecting simulation objects into flows and configuring simulation parameters. Automatic interpretation of simulation-specific JSON-FG feature properties was not tested.

              Finally, the return path was tested by publishing simulated crowd density through WMS and displaying the resulting layer in QGIS and Esri Map Viewer. This demonstrated that the simulation output could be consumed by standard geospatial clients.

              F.7 5. SCX extension

              The SCX extension developed in this research enables simulation applications to interpret the meaning of georeferenced scene layers, rather than only displaying their geometry. It builds on the lightweight SCX schema introduced by Esri Nederland in Digital Twin as a Service Testbed II, which describes a scene through a camera view, time settings, and references to external data sources.

              The extension introduces a function field that identifies the role of a layer within a scene and a preferredCrs field that identifies the coordinate reference system (CRS) in which the scene is intended to be used. It also uses JSON-FG to exchange georeferenced 2D scene layers, allowing each referenced file to state its data CRS when necessary.

              The following subsections explain these design choices and their application in the CAD-to-crowd workflow.

              F.7.1 5.1 Semantic layer functions

              The principal addition is the function field on an SCX layer. The existing type field indicates how a layer is represented or loaded, while function describes the role its data has within a particular scene. Layers containing similar polygons can therefore be interpreted differently by simulation applications.

              Because the function belongs to the scene layer rather than the source data, the same dataset can serve different purposes in different scenes. For example, an open area could be available for general pedestrian movement in one event layout but designated as a queueing area in another.

              The field accepts an open string rather than a fixed vocabulary. The crowd modeling roles considered in this research are walkable, obstacle, entry, exit, queue, and waypoint. These describe functional concepts rather than a particular application's object classes. The SimCrowds-specific interpretation of each function is provided once, in Section 5.5. In the developed importer, a layer without a recognized function is treated as visual only and does not produce a simulation object.

              F.7.2 5.2 Coordinate reference systems

              The extension distinguishes between the working CRS of a scene and the CRS of its underlying data.

              The document-level field preferredCrs identifies the CRS in which the scene is intended to be used, typically the CRS of the original design. Its value is a CRS URI, such as http://www.opengis.net/def/crs/EPSG/0/28992 for the Dutch national projected coordinate system (RD).

              This field is advisory. It does not determine how coordinates in referenced datasets are interpreted. Instead, the importer reads the CRS of each dataset and transforms the geometry to its working CRS when necessary. This allows a scene to combine data from different coordinate systems without requiring every source to be converted before export.

              Preserving the source CRS also helps avoid unnecessary coordinate transformations. Converting CAD geometry from an original projected CRS into WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84), particularly if it is later transformed back or its coordinates are rounded, may introduce small geometric discrepancies. In principle, shifts near narrow pedestrian passages or obstacle boundaries could affect a navigation mesh and consequently simulated movement.

              JSON-FG 1.0 extends GeoJSON with additional members, including coordRefSys to identify the data CRS. When coordRefSys is absent, or when a layer uses plain GeoJSON, the standard geographic coordinate reference system is WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84).

              The original SCX view section remains separate from preferredCrs. Its camera position uses latitude, longitude, and altitude regardless of the scene's preferred working CRS.

              F.7.3 5.3 Scene layer data and feature properties

              In this research, layers with type set to scene reference static JSON-FG files containing georeferenced 2D features. Each file contains a FeatureCollection with geometry and associated feature properties. Plain GeoJSON is also accepted.

              JSON-FG supports two geometry representations:

              • place contains geometry in the CRS identified by coordRefSys, allowing data to remain in a projected CRS such as RD.
              • geometry contains the GeoJSON representation in OGC:CRS84. When place is used, geometry can provide a geographic fallback for clients that do not support the alternative CRS, or it can be null.

              This arrangement allows the exporter to preserve georeferenced CAD geometry in its projected CRS while providing a geographic representation when appropriate. JSON-FG files also use the conformsTo member to identify the specification conformance classes they implement.

              Simulation-specific information can be included in the properties member of each feature. For example, an entry could have an agents_per_second property indicating an inflow rate. SCX does not standardize these parameters. The exporter determines which values to include, and the importer decides whether and how to interpret them.

              JSON-FG is an existing OGC standard, so this report does not reproduce its complete specification. Further information is available in the official JSON-FG 1.0 specification. Appendix A provides a compact example of a JSON-FG file referenced by an SCX scene layer.

              F.9 7. Discussion

              The research demonstrates how SCX can be extended beyond visualization to support the construction of simulation environments from georeferenced scene data. The CAD-to-crowd workflow illustrates the practical value of combining a lightweight scene description with semantic information, while preserving the flexibility of the original SCX approach.

              F.9.1 7.1 Semantic interoperability

              The principal contribution of the extension is the ability to describe the function of a layer within a scene. By distinguishing between geometric representation and semantic meaning, SCX allows simulation applications to interpret scene data and construct corresponding simulation objects.

              The implemented prototype demonstrates that CAD drawings can be imported into SimCrowds and used to construct a simulation environment without manually redrawing the geometry. In practice, this could save hours or days of manual work, depending on the complexity of the event site. The benefit is particularly relevant when event designs change, since updated designs can be imported without repeating the drawing process.

              Defining functions at the SCX layer level also allows the same source data to serve different purposes in different scenes. Combined with the use of open function strings, this makes the extension adaptable to different applications without introducing domain-specific requirements into the core schema.

              Simulation-specific parameters can remain in JSON-FG feature properties, allowing individual applications to exchange additional information without requiring SCX to standardize their simulation models.

              F.9.2 7.2 Coordinate reference systems

              The extension separates the preferred working CRS of a scene from the coordinate reference systems of its underlying datasets. The advisory preferredCrs communicates the intended working CRS, while each JSON-FG dataset identifies its own CRS through the standard JSON-FG conventions.

              This approach permits CAD-derived geometry to remain in a national projected CRS such as RD (EPSG:28992), without an obligatory intermediate WGS 84 conversion. For navigation models sensitive to the placement of walkable boundaries and obstacles, avoiding unnecessary transformations is a relevant design choice. The testbed did not measure changes in simulation accuracy attributable to CRS transformations.

              Using JSON-FG also builds on an existing OGC standard rather than introducing a separate coordinate reference mechanism within SCX. This preserves the lightweight character of the scene document and its separation from the referenced geographic data.

              F.9.3 7.3 Scope and limitations of the prototype

              One interoperability limitation concerns the use of open function strings. They provide flexibility but do not guarantee consistent interpretation between independent applications without shared conventions. Applications may name equivalent functions differently or interpret the same function in different ways.

              Imported objects still require connections into pedestrian flows and the configuration of simulation parameters. This is a boundary of the demonstrated automation, but not necessarily a weakness of the CAD-to-crowd workflow. Defining pedestrian flows in SCAD Re-Event would require adding simulation-specific functionality to a CAD application that is primarily designed for drawing event layouts. Keeping these steps in SimCrowds allows event designers to define the physical layout in CAD while simulation modelers configure pedestrian behavior in a tool designed for that purpose.

              F.9.4 7.4 Applicability beyond crowd simulation

              Although this research uses crowd simulation as its application, the underlying approach is not specific to pedestrian movement. Other simulation domains, such as traffic or hydraulic modeling, could define their own scene-level functions and interpret the associated geometry according to their requirements.

              By separating scene context, geographic data, and application-specific parameters, the extension provides a reusable approach for exchanging information between different types of digital twin components.

              This contributes to the objectives of the nLDT architecture by enabling independent applications to exchange meaningful spatial information through open formats, while retaining their specialized simulation capabilities.

              F.10 8. Conclusions, further research, and next steps

              F.10.1 8.1 Conclusion

              This research demonstrates that SCX can be extended to give georeferenced scene layers semantic meaning, allowing simulation applications to construct environments from externally drawn spatial data. The extension introduces a scene-level function field, an advisory preferredCrs, and the use of JSON-FG for georeferenced 2D scene data.

              The implemented CAD-to-crowd demo confirms the feasibility of this approach. Event designs exported from SCAD Re-Event were successfully imported into SimCrowds, where layer functions were interpreted to create simulation objects and generate a navigation mesh without manual redrawing.

              The automation covers construction of the simulation environment, not complete simulation configuration. Users must still connect simulation objects into flows and configure parameters before running a simulation.

              F.10.2 8.2 Further research

              The results support further development of semantic scene exchange within the SCX framework. We recommend the following research directions:

              • Application to other simulation domains: Investigate whether the extended SCX approach can support other simulation domains, such as traffic modeling and flood simulation. Testing exchanges between independent applications in these domains would help establish which semantic conventions can be shared and which require domain-specific definitions.
              • Shared function identifiers: Investigate a shared vocabulary of layer functions using URI-based identifiers. This would support the consistent interpretation of functions across different applications. For example, the function queue could be represented by a URI such as https://example.org/scx/functions/crowd-sim/queue. This approach could accommodate functions from different simulation domains while preserving extensibility and reducing ambiguity between implementations.
              • Deeper CAD-to-simulation integration: Investigate ways to maintain a continuous connection between CAD authoring tools and simulation applications. For example, a crowd simulator could retrieve updated geometry from a CAD endpoint and automatically update its simulation environment while preserving existing simulation flows, connections, and parameters. This could further reduce manual work when event designs change.

              F.10.3 8.3 Next steps

              The CAD-to-crowd SCX extension is already being used in a commercial project. The consortium will continue developing the integration between SCAD Re-Event and SimCrowds based on practical experience and findings from these projects.

              Further development can focus on reducing the remaining manual configuration steps, including the interpretation of simulation parameters from JSON-FG feature properties and the preservation of simulation configurations when CAD designs are updated.

              These activities will provide additional practical experience with semantic scene exchange and can contribute to the further development of interoperable digital twin applications within the nLDT ecosystem.

              F.11 Appendix A. Technical field reference

              This appendix summarizes the SCX extension fields and the JSON-FG structure used for georeferenced scene layers. It also provides an example from the CAD exporter.

              F.11.1 A.1 SCX document

              The extended SCX document retains the original view, time, and layers structure and adds preferredCrs at document level and function at layer level.

              Field Description
              preferredCrs URI identifying the preferred working CRS of the scene. Does not define the CRS of layer data or camera coordinates.
              view.heading Camera direction in degrees clockwise from north.
              view.tilt Camera angle in degrees, from 0° (downward) to 90° (horizontal).
              view.position Camera location as latitude, longitude (WGS 84 decimal degrees) and altitude (meters).
              time.current Selected scene time, expressed as an ISO 8601 timestamp.
              time.start, time.end Start and end of the scene's time interval, expressed as ISO 8601 timestamps.
              layers Ordered array of layer objects referencing external sources.

              SCX layer objects use the following fields:

              Field Description
              title Human-readable layer name.
              type Representation or loading category, such as scene, imagery, or elevation.
              visible Boolean controlling layer visibility.
              url URL of the referenced data source.
              function Scene-specific role, such as walkable, obstacle, entry, exit, queue, or waypoint.

              F.11.2 A.2 JSON-FG scene layer data

              In this research, scene layers reference static JSON-FG 1.0 FeatureCollections. Plain GeoJSON is also accepted.

              Member Description
              type Identifies the root object as a FeatureCollection.
              conformsTo Identifies the JSON-FG conformance classes implemented by the file.
              coordRefSys Identifies the data CRS through a URI. When absent, the default is WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84).
              features Array of geographic features.
              features[].id Feature identifier, where provided.
              features[].place Geometry in the CRS identified by coordRefSys.
              features[].geometry GeoJSON geometry in WGS 84 (OGC:CRS84), or null when geometry is provided through place.
              features[].properties Application-specific attributes, including potential simulation parameters.

              The complete format is specified in the OGC JSON-FG 1.0 specification.
